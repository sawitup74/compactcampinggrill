Backpacking Basics for Car Campers Ready to Walk In

What changes when you carry everything on your back: the big three, water, food, navigation and a first overnight plan.

At some point many car campers look at a trail disappearing into the trees and wonder what is out there. Backpacking is the answer, and it is less intimidating than it looks. You are still camping. You simply carry a smaller, lighter version of your camp. This guide covers what changes and how to plan a gentle first overnight.

The big three

Your heaviest items are shelter, sleep system and pack. Getting these lighter makes the biggest difference.

  • Shelter: a one- or two-person backpacking tent, or a tarp setup for experienced campers.
  • Sleep system: a compressible bag and an inflatable or foam pad.
  • Pack: sized to your torso, typically 40 to 65 liters for overnight to multi-day trips. Have it fitted if possible.

Water

Water is heavy, about two pounds per liter. Instead of carrying it all, plan around sources on the map and treat what you collect.

  • Filters, chemical tablets, UV pens and boiling all work for treating water.
  • Carry enough to reach the next reliable source, plus a margin.
  • In dry country, sources can vanish in summer. Ask a ranger about current conditions.

Food on the trail

Backpacking food trades fresh for light. Think calorie-dense and shelf-stable: tortillas, nut butter, hard cheese, jerky, dried fruit, instant rice and dehydrated meals. A small stove is the common backpacking cook system. Many backcountry areas restrict or ban open fires, so plan to cook without one.

Where does a grill fit? Usually at the trailhead and at basecamp-style trips. Many backpackers love a cooked steak and a small fire on the night before a hike or the night they come out. A compact grill living in the car is a great reward waiting at the end. For short walk-ins to established campsites, a lightweight steel grill can make the trip if fires are allowed, but on true multi-day treks, weight wins.

Navigation

Carry a map of the area and know how to read it. Phone apps with downloaded offline maps are helpful, but batteries die. A compass and basic skills are worth learning. Tell someone your route and return time.

Clothing layers

  • Base layer that wicks moisture
  • Insulating layer (fleece or puffy)
  • Rain shell
  • Extra socks and a warm hat
  • Avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold

The ten essentials

Hiking groups recommend carrying these ten systems on any trip into the backcountry:

  1. Navigation
  2. Headlamp
  3. Sun protection
  4. First aid
  5. Knife or multi-tool
  6. Fire starting materials
  7. Emergency shelter
  8. Extra food
  9. Extra water
  10. Extra clothes

Your first overnight

Pick a trail of two to five miles with a known campsite, modest elevation gain, and water nearby. Go in good weather. Start early so you arrive with plenty of daylight to set up camp. Many state parks have walk-in or primitive sites that make excellent first backpacking trips; ask the park about permits and fire rules.

Leave No Trace matters more out here

Backcountry areas get fewer cleanup crews. Pack out everything, including food scraps. Use existing campsites. Dig catholes far from water. Keep fires small or skip them entirely where rules or conditions advise.

Trailhead feast

Here is a tradition worth starting: leave a flat-pack grill, charcoal, a lighter and a cooler of something good in the car. When you come off the trail tired and hungry, a hot meal at the trailhead parking area (where permitted) or your next campground is the best part of the trip.

Questions campers ask

How heavy should my backpacking pack be?

Many hikers aim for a total weight around 20 percent of body weight or less for comfort, though it varies.

Can I have a campfire while backpacking?

It depends on the area. Many backcountry zones restrict fires; check rules and conditions.

Is a grill practical for backpacking?

For long treks, usually not. For short walk-ins to established sites or trailhead meals, a compact grill can be worth it.
