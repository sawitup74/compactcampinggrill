Camping Games and Activities for Kids and Families

Campsite games, nature activities and fireside fun for all ages, with no screens required.

Half the magic of family camping is the unstructured time: hours with nothing scheduled but exploring. Still, a few games in your pocket can turn a restless afternoon or a long evening into a highlight. Here are tried-and-true camping activities.

Daytime games

Nature scavenger hunt

Make a list: something smooth, something with a smell, a feather, a pinecone, a red leaf, something a bird made. Kids find and photograph items, leaving them in place.

Bird bingo

Make bingo cards with local birds. Bring binoculars and a field guide.

Leaf rubbings

Paper and crayons over leaves create beautiful prints.

Bug safari

Use a magnifying glass to look at insects and spiders. Look, don't touch.

Trail detectives

Look for tracks, scat, chewed nuts and nests. Make up stories about who passed by.

Evening and fireside fun

Storytelling chain

One person starts a story, each person adds a sentence.

Shadow puppets

Use a lantern or headlamp and the side of the tent.

Stargazing

Find the Big Dipper and the North Star. Download a star map before you go. Many parks in West Texas are famous for dark skies.

Camp songs

Songs with call-and-response are perfect for kids.

Cooking as an activity

Cooking at camp is a hands-on activity kids love. Older kids can help with prep, flipping pancakes on a griddle, or assembling foil-free skewers. Toddlers can help season, stir cold ingredients, or set the table.

A compact grill sits low enough for kids to see the cooking but with clear boundaries. Teach them that the grill and everything around it is hot, always.

Rainy-day ideas

  • Cards and board games under the tarp
  • Reading aloud
  • Drawing and journaling
  • A rain walk in rain jackets

Junior ranger programs

Many state and national parks offer junior ranger activity books. Kids complete activities and earn a badge. Ask at the visitor center.

Leave No Trace games

Make "leave what you find" a challenge. Turn trash pick-up into a race. Kids who learn these values early carry them for life.

Activities by age

Toddlers (1 to 3)

  • Splashing in shallow, supervised water
  • Collecting leaves and pinecones in a bucket
  • Pointing out birds, squirrels and bugs
  • Simple songs and clapping games

Preschool (3 to 5)

  • Color scavenger hunts: find something green, yellow, brown
  • Nature bracelets made with tape, sticky side out, to collect small fallen leaves and petals
  • Animal track guessing
  • Flashlight tag at dusk in a cleared area

Elementary (6 to 10)

  • Nature bingo and scavenger hunts
  • Junior ranger books
  • Learning to identify three trees
  • Helping plan and cook a meal
  • Simple knots like the square knot and bowline

Tweens and teens

  • Map and compass navigation challenges
  • Planning a day hike
  • Building and tending the fire, with supervision
  • Cooking a full meal for the family
  • Night sky photography
  • Fishing with a license as required

Classic campfire games

Two truths and a lie

Each person tells three "facts" about themselves; others guess the lie.

Would you rather

Ask silly questions: would you rather camp with a bear or a moose?

Twenty questions

One person thinks of an animal or object; others ask yes or no questions.

Camp trivia

Prepare questions about the park, wildlife, or the stars.

Night sky activities

  • Find the Big Dipper and follow its pointer stars to the North Star.
  • Watch for satellites, which move steadily across the sky.
  • Look for meteors during known meteor showers.
  • Let eyes adjust in the dark for about 20 minutes.
  • Use red light headlamps to preserve night vision.

Water activities

With life jackets and supervision:

  • Skipping stones
  • Building small dams with sand (then removing them)
  • Catching and releasing tadpoles with a net
  • Kayaking or canoeing in calm water

Making cooking a game

  • Chopped camp edition: give older kids a basket of ingredients and challenge them to make a dish.
  • Pancake art: pour shapes on the griddle.
  • Build your own pizza on tortillas, toasted on a griddle.

Quiet time activities

  • Nature journals
  • Reading in the hammock
  • Watercolor painting
  • Cloud watching

Packing a fun kit

Bring a small bin with cards, a ball, a frisbee, a magnifying glass, field guides, journals, crayons and a deck of animal facts.

Games that need no gear

  • Twenty Questions: one person thinks of an animal, plant or object spotted that day; others ask yes-or-no questions.
  • Story circle: each person adds one sentence to a story that goes around the fire.
  • Would you rather: silly or thoughtful choices that get everyone talking.
  • I spy: perfect for younger kids in daylight.
  • Animal sounds: guess the animal from an imitation.
  • Two truths and a lie: great for groups who are still getting to know each other.

Games with simple gear

  • Cards: a waterproof deck handles damp evenings.
  • Dice games: small, packable and good for all ages.
  • Frisbee: open fields or beaches.
  • Cornhole or ring toss: for car camping when space allows.
  • Glow-in-the-dark ball games after dark in open areas away from the fire.

Nature activities

  • Scavenger hunt: pine cone, smooth rock, feather, something red, animal tracks (look, photograph, don't collect where prohibited).
  • Bird watching: bring binoculars and a field guide.
  • Bug safari: look under logs (and put them back as you found them).
  • Leaf rubbing: paper and crayons.
  • Cloud spotting: lie back and name shapes.
  • Junior Ranger programs: many parks offer them; ask at the visitor center.

Night activities

  • Stargazing: let eyes adjust for 20 minutes away from lights, and use a red light for maps.
  • Night hike: short and on a known trail, with headlamps.
  • Listening game: sit silently for two minutes and count different sounds.
  • Shadow puppets on the tent wall.

Campfire time

When fires are allowed, the evening fire is the heart of camp. A grill that works as a raised fire pit keeps the fire contained and up off the ground, and the same steel that cooked dinner becomes the evening's gathering point. Tell stories, sing songs, or just watch the coals.

Rainy day plans

  • Card and board games in the tent or under a tarp.
  • Reading and journaling.
  • Visiting a nearby town, museum or visitor center.
  • Puddle stomping in rain gear (warm weather only).

Activities for teens

Teens enjoy responsibility. Let them plan a meal, build the fire under supervision, navigate a hike or set up the tent. Give them some freedom within clear limits.

Keeping activities low impact

  • Stay on trails.
  • Don't pick plants or move rocks in parks where it's prohibited.
  • Keep noise down during quiet hours.
  • Leave natural objects where you find them.

Campfire songs and stories

Every family and group develops its own campfire traditions. Pick a few easy sing-along songs everyone knows, or pass a flashlight and tell stories in turn. For kids, keep stories funny or mysterious rather than truly scary, so they still want to sleep in the tent. Older kids and adults often enjoy legends tied to the place you're camping; ask a ranger about local history.

Activities around the grill

Cooking can be an activity too. Let everyone build their own foil packet, skewer or pizza, then cook them together. Kids are more likely to eat food they helped make.

Photo challenge

Give each person a list: a sunrise, a bug up close, a reflection in water, a funny face, the camp cook in action. Compare photos at the fire.

Quiet time ideas

Not every camp activity needs to be active. Bring a hammock, a book, sketchbook or journal, and let everyone have an hour of quiet in the afternoon. Kids who rest midday often handle late nights around the fire much better.

Planning activities by age

Mix activities so every age has something to enjoy. Younger kids like scavenger hunts and simple games, older kids like challenges and responsibility, and adults like quiet time and good conversation. Rotate leadership so everyone gets to pick an activity.

Morning activities

Early mornings are quiet and magical at camp. Take a sunrise walk, look for wildlife near water, or sit with coffee and listen to birds. Kids who wake early can help start the breakfast coals with an adult.

Questions campers ask

What can kids do while camping?

Scavenger hunts, nature exploration, stargazing, storytelling and cooking are all favorites.

Are there junior ranger programs in parks?

Many state and national parks offer junior ranger activities. Ask at the visitor center.

What should we do when it rains at camp?

Play games under a tarp, read, draw or take a walk in rain gear.
