Kayak Camping: How to Pack a Boat for an Overnight Trip

Choosing a kayak, packing dry bags, picking campsites and cooking on a river or lake trip.

Kayak camping combines two of the best outdoor experiences: paddling to places cars can't reach and camping where the only sounds are water and birds. It also requires smart packing, because a kayak holds a surprising amount of gear but only if you use the space well. Here is how to plan and pack your first trip.

Choosing the boat

  • Touring and sea kayaks have sealed bulkhead hatches and long hulls that track straight. Great for lakes, bays and long distances.
  • Recreational sit-in kayaks are stable and roomy but often lack bulkheads. Use flotation bags and dry bags.
  • Sit-on-top kayaks are easy to get on and off and popular in warm water. Gear rides in tank wells strapped down.
  • Canoes carry far more gear and are classic for river trips with two paddlers.

Packing principles

  1. Everything in dry bags. Assume water will get in.
  2. Small bags fit better than big ones. Several small dry bags fill odd-shaped hatches.
  3. Heavy items low and centered, near the cockpit, to keep the boat stable.
  4. Light, bulky items at the ends, like sleeping bags.
  5. Essentials on deck or in reach: water, snacks, sunscreen, map, pump, and a rain layer.
  6. Balance side to side.

Gear checklist for a paddle camp

  • PFD worn at all times on the water
  • Spare paddle
  • Bilge pump and paddle float for sit-ins
  • Whistle
  • Dry bags of several sizes
  • Tent, sleeping bag and pad
  • Water and treatment
  • Food in a hard container to protect it from crushing and critters
  • Compact cooking setup
  • First aid kit
  • Headlamp
  • Map and float plan left with someone at home

Cooking on a paddle trip

Flat items pack best in kayaks, which is why a flat-pack grill is a natural fit. The Slot Grill Compact packs down to about an inch thick and is often packed in kayaks, trunks, RVs and camp boxes. At about 14 lb, it is heavier than a backpacking stove, so think about your boat, trip length and how much you want fresh food versus dehydrated.

Where fires are allowed at your campsite, the grill gives you a clean, raised fire container on sandbars or shorelines. That matters on rivers, where you might otherwise be scraping together a fire on someone's old blackened rock ring full of fishing line and foil.

Choosing a campsite on the water

  • Know the rules: some rivers have designated sites, others allow sandbar camping, and private land along rivers may be off limits.
  • Pick high ground well above the water line. Rivers can rise overnight from rain upstream.
  • Pull boats well up and tie them off.
  • Check for shade, wind exposure and level tent spots.

Safety on the water

  • Always wear your PFD.
  • Check weather and water levels before launching.
  • Dress for water temperature, not air temperature.
  • Paddle with others and know self-rescue techniques.
  • Leave a float plan with a friend.

Food plan for a two-day paddle

  • Day one dinner: fresh chicken or sausage on the grill, frozen solid at the start of the trip.
  • Day two breakfast: tortillas, eggs or a griddle breakfast.
  • Lunch both days: wraps, cheese, jerky, fruit.
  • Snacks you can reach from the cockpit.

Leave No Trace on the water

Pack out all trash, including fishing line. Strain dishwater. Use existing campsites on shores and sandbars. Drown fires and pack out cold ash rather than leaving black marks on sand.

Choosing your first kayak camping route

  • Calm water such as a lake or slow river.
  • Short distance between launch and camp.
  • Known campsites.
  • Easy shuttle or out-and-back.
  • Good weather forecast.

Rescue skills

Practice wet exits, assisted rescues and self-rescue with a paddle float before a trip.

Troubleshooting

  • Boat tipping: too much weight up high.
  • Wet gear: improper dry bags.
  • Sore back: adjust seat and footpegs.

Choosing a kayak for camping

  • Sit-on-top kayaks: stable, easy to re-enter and common in warm southern waters, with deck space for strapping gear.
  • Sit-inside touring kayaks: faster and drier, with sealed hatches for gear, better suited to longer distances.
  • Recreational kayaks: short and stable, good for calm water and short overnight trips with limited gear.
  • Inflatable kayaks: pack small for transport but are slower and more affected by wind.

Learning skills before you camp

Practice on day trips first: loading a full boat, paddling it in wind, self-rescue and re-entry. A loaded kayak handles very differently from an empty one, turning more slowly and sitting lower.

Packing smaller and lighter

Kayak space is tight, so every item earns its place.

  • Choose compressible sleeping bags and pads.
  • Repack food into soft bags and remove bulky packaging at home.
  • Bring a flat-pack grill that slides into a hatch or straps flat on deck.
  • Use small, separate dry bags rather than one huge one; they fit hatches better.

Balancing the load

  1. Heavy gear and water go low and close to the cockpit.
  2. Keep weight balanced side to side.
  3. Pack light, bulky items toward the ends.
  4. Do a test float before leaving shore.

Choosing a campsite from the water

  • Look for designated sites or public land where camping is allowed.
  • Land on sand or gravel, not on vegetated banks.
  • Pull boats well above the waterline and tie them off; water can rise overnight from rain upstream or tides.
  • Set camp on durable ground away from the edge.

Weather on open water

Wind is the biggest danger for kayakers. Afternoon winds can make crossings rough and dangerous. Paddle early, hug the shoreline, and be ready to wait out wind on shore. Get off the water when thunder rolls.

Cooking from a kayak camp

Keep meals simple, but don't settle for cold food every night. A light grill on a durable surface lets you cook fresh meat the first night and foil packs or griddle meals after. Keep the cooking area well above the waterline and clear of vegetation.

Leave no trace from the water

  • Pack out everything, including food scraps.
  • Use restrooms where available; otherwise follow local waste rules.
  • Wash dishes away from the water.
  • Never dump ash or grease into water.

Kayak camping checklist highlights

  • Life jacket, paddle, spare paddle
  • Bilge pump and sponge
  • Dry bags
  • Whistle and light
  • Tow line
  • Map and compass or GPS
  • Sun protection
  • Repair kit

Day-one checklist at the launch

Before you push off, confirm your plan with someone at home, check the weather one last time, double-check that every hatch is sealed and every bag tied in, and do a short test paddle near shore to feel how the loaded boat handles. Adjust trim if the bow or stern sits too low.

Paddling with a partner

Paddle within sight and hearing of each other, agree on signals, and stop together for breaks. Practice assisted rescues before the trip so you know what to do if someone capsizes.

Kayak cooking menu

  • Night one: steak or chicken from the cooler.
  • Night two: foil packs with sausage and vegetables.
  • Breakfast: griddle eggs and tortillas.

Choosing your first route

For a first kayak camping trip, choose calm, protected water such as a small lake or slow river with designated campsites a short paddle from the launch. Two to three hours of paddling a day is plenty when the boat is loaded. Look for routes with easy landings, where you can bail out if weather turns. As your skills grow, try longer routes, open bays or multi-day river trips, always checking local rules for camping and fires.

Drying out at camp

At the end of each paddling day, string a line between trees for wet clothes and towels, open hatches to air them, and change into dry clothes right away. Damp gear dries faster in morning sun and breeze.

A final note

Always paddle within your limits.

Questions campers ask

How do I keep gear dry in a kayak?

Pack everything in dry bags and assume water will get into hatches.

Can I bring a grill on a kayak camping trip?

Yes. A flat-pack grill fits well in kayaks. Consider weight, boat size and fire rules.

Where can I camp on a river?

It depends on the river. Some have designated sites, others allow sandbar camping; private land may be off limits.
