Camping in the Big Bend Region of Texas

Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park and the Davis Mountains: desert camping, dark skies and planning for remote country.

Far West Texas is a different world: Chihuahuan Desert, rugged mountains, the Rio Grande, and some of the darkest skies in the country. Camping here is unforgettable, but it requires preparation. Services are far apart, water is scarce, and weather can be extreme.

Big Bend National Park

The national park sits in the big bend of the Rio Grande. Its landscapes range from desert floor to the Chisos Mountains, which rise in the park's center, to the river canyons such as Santa Elena Canyon. The park has developed campgrounds and backcountry camping by permit.

Big Bend Ranch State Park

West of the national park, Big Bend Ranch is one of the largest state parks in Texas. It is remote and rugged, with primitive camping, backcountry roads and trails.

Davis Mountains State Park

North of the Big Bend region near Fort Davis, the Davis Mountains offer higher elevations, cooler temperatures and grasslands. The McDonald Observatory nearby hosts star parties.

Dark skies

The region is known for exceptional stargazing. Plan around the new moon for the darkest nights.

Planning essentials

  • Fill fuel tanks at every opportunity.
  • Carry plenty of water, more than you think you need.
  • Check road conditions; some backcountry roads require high-clearance vehicles.
  • Cell service is limited.
  • Summer heat on the desert floor can be dangerous; spring, fall and winter are popular seasons.

Fire and cooking

Fire rules in the desert are strict because vegetation is fragile and fires spread fast. Many areas ban ground fires entirely, and backcountry rules may limit what is allowed. Check current regulations before you go.

Where your type of grill is permitted, a raised steel grill keeps coals contained and off the desert floor. Pack out all ash and charcoal; nothing should be left behind.

Wildlife

Expect javelinas, roadrunners, and many bird species. Store food securely; javelinas and other animals are known to raid unattended camps.

Leave No Trace

Desert soils and plants recover slowly. Stay on durable surfaces and pack out everything.

Desert camping skills

Water planning

The desert is the one place where you must carry all the water you need. A common planning figure is at least a gallon per person per day for drinking and cooking, and more in heat or when hiking. Keep extra in the vehicle. Water sources in the backcountry are unreliable.

Heat management

  • Hike early in the morning and late in the afternoon.
  • Rest in shade at midday.
  • Wear a wide-brimmed hat and light, long sleeves.
  • Watch for signs of heat illness.

Cold nights

Deserts lose heat quickly after sunset. Nights can be surprisingly cold, especially in winter and at higher elevations like the Chisos Basin. Pack warm layers.

Wind and dust

Wind can kick up dust storms. Stake tents firmly and keep gear secured.

Desert hazards

  • Flash floods: never camp in a dry wash or arroyo.
  • Rattlesnakes and scorpions: shake out boots, watch where you step and place your hands.
  • Cactus spines: bring tweezers.
  • Remoteness: tell someone your plans; carry a paper map.

Stargazing tips

Big Bend is famous for dark skies. For the best viewing:

  1. Plan your trip around the new moon.
  2. Let your eyes adjust for about 20 minutes.
  3. Use a red light.
  4. Lie back on a pad or reclining chair.
  5. Look for the Milky Way, planets and meteor showers.

Photography

Desert light is beautiful at sunrise and sunset. Bring extra batteries; cold nights drain them.

Cooking in the desert

With strict fire rules, cooking in the desert needs planning. Where your grill type is allowed, set it on a durable surface, keep it small, and never leave it unattended. Desert winds can carry embers far.

Pack all cooking waste out. Desert soil doesn't break down food scraps quickly, and scraps attract animals.

Menu for desert trips

  • Breakfast burritos on a griddle
  • Grilled chicken fajitas
  • Chili for cold nights
  • Fresh fruit kept cold in the cooler early, then dried fruit later

Driving tips

  • Fill up fuel whenever possible.
  • Carry a spare tire and know how to change it.
  • Avoid driving at night when wildlife is active on roads.
  • Respect closures; some backcountry roads become impassable after rain.

Border region awareness

The Rio Grande forms the international border. Follow park guidance and posted rules about river access and crossings.

Planning around distance

Big Bend country is remote. Distances between services are long, and cell coverage is limited in much of the region. Fill the tank whenever you can, carry extra water, and download maps before you leave.

Seasons in the desert

  • Late fall through early spring: the most popular camping season, with mild days and cold nights.
  • Late spring and summer: extremely hot at lower elevations; higher mountain areas are cooler.
  • Summer rains: monsoon-season storms can bring flash flooding in washes.

Desert water rules of thumb

Carry far more water than you expect to use, especially on hikes. Plan at least a gallon per person per day for camp, plus extra for cooking and emergencies. Don't count on finding water in the backcountry.

Desert camp setup

  • Pick sites with natural wind breaks.
  • Stake tents well; desert winds can be strong.
  • Watch for washes and never camp in one; flash floods come quickly.
  • Shake out boots and clothing before putting them on.

Cooking in the desert

Desert air is dry, and wind can carry sparks. Always follow park fire regulations, which can be strict in desert parks. Where charcoal is allowed, cook in a raised grill in a cleared area and keep water nearby.

Night skies

The Big Bend region is famous for dark night skies. Plan to stay up at least one night to watch the stars. Use red lights to preserve night vision.

Wildlife

Watch for javelinas, coyotes and other desert animals. Store food securely, as javelinas and other animals can raid unsecured coolers. Keep a respectful distance from all wildlife.

Desert hiking safety

  • Hike early or late in the day.
  • Carry plenty of water and salty snacks.
  • Wear sun protection.
  • Tell someone your route.
  • Turn back when you've used half your water.

Leaving the desert clean

Desert soils and plants recover very slowly. Stay on established roads and trails, camp at established sites, and pack out all trash.

Packing for big temperature swings

Desert temperatures can swing widely from day to night. Pack sun protection for the day and warm layers for the night.

Cooking tips

Wind is common. Use a windbreak and keep the grill clear of brush.

Photography

Golden hour light on desert landscapes is spectacular. Get out early and late.

Choosing where to camp in the region

The Big Bend area offers developed campgrounds, primitive roadside sites and backcountry camping, each with its own rules and permit requirements. Developed campgrounds have restrooms and water, while primitive sites have few or no services. Check the official park sites for current permit and reservation requirements before you go, and pick sites that match your vehicle's clearance and your comfort with remote travel.

Vehicle preparation

Some roads in the region are rough and remote. Check tires including the spare, carry extra water and fuel, and know your vehicle's limits. Ask rangers about current road conditions.

A simple desert camp menu

Desert trips reward meals that need little water to prepare or clean up. Night one can be fajitas with pre-sliced meat and peppers seared on the griddle, wrapped in tortillas warmed on the grate. Night two works well as foil-pack chicken and potatoes, which leave almost nothing to wash. Breakfasts can be breakfast tacos with eggs and chorizo, and lunches can be wraps, jerky, cheese and fruit that need no cooking at all. Keep the cooler in the shade of the vehicle, drain meltwater daily, and add ice whenever you pass a store, since the next one may be far away. Wipe the grill with a dry towel and a little oil rather than washing it, which saves precious water for drinking.

One more tip

Above all, respect the remoteness and plan carefully; it rewards you with unforgettable quiet.

Questions campers ask

When is the best time to camp in Big Bend?

Spring, fall and winter are popular; summer on the desert floor can be extremely hot.

Can I have a campfire in Big Bend National Park?

Ground fires are generally prohibited. Check the park's current rules for grills and stoves.

What is Big Bend known for?

Desert and mountain landscapes, the Rio Grande canyons and exceptional dark skies.
