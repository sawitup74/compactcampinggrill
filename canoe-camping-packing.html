Canoe Camping: Packing, Portages and Paddle-In Meals

How to pack a canoe for an overnight trip and cook well at a riverside or lakeside camp.

Canoes carry far more than kayaks, making them ideal for comfortable paddle camping.

Packing a canoe

  • Use large dry bags or barrels.
  • Keep weight low and centered.
  • Tie gear in so it stays with the boat if you capsize.
  • Keep essentials in reach.

Portages

If you need to carry the canoe and gear between waters, pack fewer, larger bags.

Meals

Campsites

Pick high ground well above the water, and pull the canoe up and tie it.

Safety

Wear PFDs, check weather and water levels, and leave a float plan.

Choosing a canoe for camping trips

A tandem canoe in the 16 to 17 foot range is the classic tripping boat: long enough to track well across a lake, roomy enough for two people and several days of gear. Shorter boats turn quicker on twisty rivers but carry less. Solo canoes exist and are a joy for experienced paddlers, though they ask more of your packing discipline. If you are renting, tell the outfitter where you plan to go and how many nights; they will steer you to a hull that suits the water.

Materials matter mostly for weight and durability. Heavier hulls shrug off rocky shoals and shallow gravel; lighter ones make portages far kinder to your shoulders. For a first trip, any sound, well-maintained rental is fine.

The packing order that works

  1. Lay out every item on a tarp at home and remove anything you will not truly use.
  2. Group gear into bags by job: sleep, kitchen, food, clothing, safety.
  3. Put the heaviest bags (food and water) in the center of the boat, just ahead of and behind the yoke.
  4. Place bulky, light bags (sleeping bags, pads) toward the ends.
  5. Keep a small "day bag" between the stern paddler's knees with rain gear, snacks, sunscreen, map, water treatment and the first aid kit.
  6. Clip or tie every bag to a thwart with a short line so a capsize does not scatter your camp downstream.
  7. Trim the boat: with both paddlers seated, it should sit level or very slightly lower in the stern.

A sample two-night canoe gear list

  • Canoe, three paddles (one spare), PFD for each paddler
  • Bailer or sponge and a throw bag
  • Two or three large dry bags plus a few small ones
  • Tent, sleeping bags and pads
  • Cooler (first two days) and a hard food container
  • Flat-pack grill, charcoal in a sealed bucket, lighter and matches
  • Small pot, skillet, utensils, plates and mugs
  • Water filter or tablets and two or more water bottles
  • Headlamps, first aid kit, sunscreen, bug spray
  • Rain gear and a warm layer for each person
  • Map in a waterproof case and a float plan left at home
  • Trash bags and a mesh litter bag

Paddle-in meal plan

Canoe trips let you eat better than almost any other paddle trip because weight and bulk are less of a problem.

  • Night one: steak or chicken thighs frozen at home, grilled over charcoal with skewered potatoes.
  • Breakfast: griddle pancakes or a sausage and potato skillet.
  • Lunch: tortillas, hard cheese, summer sausage and fruit, eaten on a sandbar.
  • Night two: shelf-stable backup like pasta with jarred sauce, or fresh fish if you caught and kept it legally.
  • Last morning: oatmeal and coffee, then pack and paddle out.

A flat-pack grill lies along the bottom of the hull under the bags, so it takes almost no usable space. At camp, set it on bare sand, gravel or rock well above the waterline, where fires are permitted.

Portage technique

If your route includes portages, pack fewer, larger bags so you can move everything in as few trips as possible. One person carries the canoe overturned on the shoulders using the yoke; the other carries the heaviest pack and loose items like paddles. Walk the trail first if it is unfamiliar. Never rush a portage; twisted ankles end trips.

Wind, waves and big water

Lakes can turn rough quickly when wind rises. Paddle early in the morning when water is calmer, stay near shore, and cross large open stretches only when conditions are settled. Kneeling lowers your center of gravity in choppy water. If waves begin coming over the gunwales, head for shore and wait it out. Rivers have their own hazards: strainers (fallen trees that water passes through), low-head dams and fast bends. Scout anything you cannot see the end of.

Camp setup on the shore

  • Unload, then carry the canoe well above the high water mark and flip it over.
  • Tie it to a tree or a heavy rock, especially if storms are possible.
  • Pitch the tent on durable ground away from the water's edge and away from your cook area.
  • Set the kitchen downwind of the tent and at least 15 feet away.
  • Hang or secure food as local rules require.

Troubleshooting common canoe camping problems

  • Boat keeps turning: check trim; too much weight in the bow makes steering hard.
  • Gear got wet: you trusted a non-dry bag. Wring out, sun-dry at camp, and double-bag electronics next time.
  • Fire won't light on a damp shore: carry starter cubes and dry charcoal in a sealed container. Wet driftwood is a poor fuel.
  • Shoulders aching: switch sides often and use your torso, not just your arms, for each stroke.

Leave the shoreline as you found it

Shorelines take heavy use. Camp on established sites, pack out every scrap including fishing line, and never leave a fire scar on a sandbar. Carry ash out in a metal can once it is completely cold.

Packing a canoe

  • Heavy gear low and centered.
  • Balanced side to side.
  • Tie everything in.
  • Use dry bags.

Portages

Pack for portages: fewer, larger packs are easier to carry.

Food

Pack food in waterproof containers.

Cooking

A flat-pack grill fits easily in a canoe. Set it on a durable surface at camp.

Safety

Wear life jackets, carry a bailer, and know self-rescue.

Camp

Land on durable surfaces and pull canoes above the waterline.

Waterproofing everything

Assume everything in the canoe will get wet at some point. Pack clothes and sleeping bags in dry bags, use a waterproof map case, and keep electronics in sealed pouches. Line packs with heavy trash bags for an extra layer.

Packing list highlights

  • Canoe, paddles and a spare paddle
  • Life jackets
  • Bailer and sponge
  • Throw rope
  • Dry bags
  • Tent and sleeping gear
  • Kitchen kit with a flat-pack grill
  • Water filter
  • Map and compass
  • First aid kit

Loading at the shore

Load the canoe while it floats, not on land, to avoid scraping the hull. Steady it alongside the shore and step in at the center.

Cooking on a canoe trip

Plan simple meals that use fresh food early and shelf-stable food later. A flat-pack grill packs at the bottom of the canoe or in a pack and sets up in about a minute at camp. Choose a durable, non-flammable spot well above the waterline and follow fire rules.

Unloading at camp

Unload at the water's edge one pack at a time, then carry the canoe well above the waterline and flip it over. Tie it to a tree if wind or rising water is possible.

Portage tips

Pack so each person carries one load per trip on portages. Use packs with good shoulder straps, and practice lifting and carrying the canoe before the trip. A flat-pack grill rides easily in a pack between other gear.

Coming home

Rinse and dry the canoe, dry bags and tent, clean and oil the grill, and restock the kitchen kit. Note what worked and what didn't for next time.

A final note

Label each pack by contents.

Questions campers ask

How should I pack a canoe?

Use dry bags or barrels, keep weight low and centered, and tie gear in.

Can I bring a cooler on a canoe trip?

Yes. Canoes have room for a cooler on shorter trips.

What is a portage?

Carrying your canoe and gear over land between bodies of water.

How do I trim a loaded canoe?

Put heavy bags in the center and adjust until the boat sits level or slightly lower at the stern with both paddlers seated.

What should I do if wind picks up on a lake?

Head for shore, stay close to the bank, and wait for calmer water rather than crossing open stretches.
