Texas State Parks for Camping: A Region-by-Region Guide

Hill Country rivers, Panhandle canyons, Piney Woods lakes and coastal parks: where to camp in Texas and what each region does best.

Texas is big enough that "camping in Texas" can mean a cypress swamp, a red rock canyon, a granite dome or a windswept beach, sometimes in the same week. The state park system covers all of it. This guide walks region by region through well-known parks and what they are loved for. Amenities, fees, and reservation rules change, so always check with Texas Parks and Wildlife before you go.

Hill Country

The Hill Country is the classic Texas camping region: limestone hills, clear spring-fed rivers, and live oaks.

Garner State Park

On the Frio River west of San Antonio, Garner is famous for summer tubing, swimming in cool green water, and a long tradition of summer evening dances at the pavilion. It is one of the most popular parks in the state, so plan ahead.

Enchanted Rock State Natural Area

A huge pink granite dome rising above the Hill Country north of Fredericksburg. Hikers climb the summit for sunsets and stargazing. The area is popular enough that day-use access can fill up on busy days.

Lost Maples State Natural Area

Known for fall color from its stands of bigtooth maples in the canyons of the Sabinal River. Autumn weekends are busy; spring and winter are quieter and still beautiful.

Pedernales Falls State Park

The Pedernales River tumbles over tilted limestone ledges. Be aware that the river can rise quickly after rain upstream.

Colorado Bend State Park

Known for Gorman Falls, a spring-fed waterfall, plus caves, river access and hiking and biking trails.

Inks Lake State Park

Pink granite shoreline on a constant-level lake in the Highland Lakes chain, popular for swimming, paddling and fishing.

Panhandle and Plains

Palo Duro Canyon State Park

Often described as the second-largest canyon in the United States, Palo Duro offers colorful canyon walls, the Lighthouse rock formation, and the long-running outdoor musical "TEXAS" in summer.

Caprock Canyons State Park

Red rock canyons and the official Texas State Bison Herd roaming the park.

Piney Woods and East Texas

Caddo Lake State Park

Bald cypress trees draped in Spanish moss, paddling trails through bayous, and a mysterious, quiet atmosphere. A paddler's favorite.

Tyler State Park

Pine forest around a spring-fed lake with trails for hiking and mountain biking.

Martin Dies Jr. State Park

Swamp and forest along a reservoir, known for paddling trails and wildlife.

North Texas

Dinosaur Valley State Park

Dinosaur tracks preserved in the bed of the Paluxy River near Glen Rose, visible when water levels allow.

Gulf Coast

Mustang Island State Park

Miles of beach on a barrier island near Corpus Christi, with beach camping and a paddling trail on the bay side.

Goose Island State Park

Bay-front camping near Rockport, home to a famous ancient live oak known as the Big Tree.

Galveston Island State Park

Beach and bay habitats on the same island, popular for birding, fishing and paddling.

Far West Texas

Big Bend Ranch State Park

Vast, rugged, remote Chihuahuan Desert country along the Rio Grande. It is wild and requires careful planning for water, fuel and navigation.

Davis Mountains State Park

A cooler mountain retreat near Fort Davis, close to the McDonald Observatory and known for dark skies.

Camp cooking in Texas parks

Fire rules vary between parks and change with drought. Many Texas parks allow fires only in provided rings or grills, and county burn bans can affect what is allowed. Check before your trip.

If charcoal cooking is allowed in your own container, bringing a clean, raised steel grill is a smart move. Park grills see heavy use, especially in busy parks like Garner, and there is no telling how they were treated by the last group. Your own grill means your own clean cooking surface.

Planning tips

  • Reserve early for popular parks and holiday weekends.
  • Summer heat is intense; Hill Country rivers and higher West Texas elevations offer relief.
  • Spring and fall are prime camping seasons across most of the state.
  • Download maps; cell service is limited in many parks.

Camping types in Texas state parks

Texas state parks offer a range of campsites, which can include sites with water and electricity, sites with water only, primitive walk-in sites, equestrian sites, cabins and screened shelters depending on the park. Check each park for what is available.

Reservations

Popular parks fill quickly on weekends and holidays. Reserve through the Texas Parks and Wildlife reservation system and check cancellation policies.

Seasonal highlights

  • Spring: wildflowers across the Hill Country.
  • Summer: river parks like Garner.
  • Fall: color at Lost Maples and East Texas parks.
  • Winter: Big Bend region and the coast.

Programs and activities

Many parks offer ranger programs, junior ranger activities, fishing equipment loans, and guided hikes.

Fire tips

Many parks have fire rings and grills. Check fire rules and burn bans, which change often.

Making reservations

Texas State Parks take reservations online and by phone, and popular parks fill quickly on weekends and holidays. Book early, especially for spring and fall weekends. Check the official site for current reservation rules.

Park passes

Day-use entrance fees apply at most parks, and an annual pass can save money if you visit often. Check the official site for current pass options and prices.

Types of campsites

Many parks offer a mix of sites, from primitive hike-in to full hookup RV sites, plus cabins at some parks. Read site descriptions carefully when reserving.

Seasons in Texas

  • Spring: wildflowers, mild temps, busy weekends.
  • Summer: hot, especially inland. Head for water.
  • Fall: cooler and pleasant.
  • Winter: mild in the south, cold snaps up north.

Burn bans

Texas counties issue burn bans during dry periods, and parks follow local restrictions. Check current conditions on arrival.

Wildlife

Texas parks are home to deer, armadillos, raccoons, birds and more. Store food securely and keep your distance.

Leave No Trace

Pack out trash, stay on trails, and respect wildlife.

Cooking at Texas parks

Bring your own grill for a clean cooking surface. Set it on the pad or a durable surface away from vegetation and follow park fire rules.

Choosing a Texas state park by region

  • Hill Country: rivers, limestone bluffs, swimming holes and spring wildflowers.
  • Piney Woods: tall pines, lakes and shady trails in East Texas.
  • Gulf Coast: beaches, bays, birding and fishing.
  • Panhandle Plains: canyons and wide-open skies.
  • Big Bend Country: desert and mountain landscapes in the far west.
  • Prairies and Lakes: reservoirs, fishing and family camping in central and north Texas.

Packing for Texas heat

Summer camping in much of Texas means intense heat. Pick shaded sites, carry extra water, plan activities for early mornings and evenings, and choose parks with swimming. Electric sites with fans help many campers sleep through hot nights.

Cooking at Texas parks

Most campsites have a picnic table and a fire ring or grill. Bringing your own grill gives you a clean cooking surface. Place it on the pad or another durable surface away from dry grass, follow any burn ban rules, and keep water nearby.

Firewood

Gathering firewood is generally not allowed in Texas state parks; buy wood locally or bring charcoal for your grill where fires are permitted.

Junior Ranger and programs

Many parks offer ranger programs and activities for kids. Ask at headquarters or check the park's event calendar.

Making the most of a short stay

Many Texas campers do quick one- or two-night trips. To make the most of them, arrive early enough to set up in daylight, choose a park within a couple of hours of home, and pick one main activity, like a hike, a paddle or a swim, rather than trying to do everything. Prep meals at home so cooking at the site is fast, and let the evening around the grill be the centerpiece of the trip. Short trips done often build skills faster than a single long trip each year.

One more tip

Check each park's page for alerts before you go, since conditions can change.

Questions campers ask

What is the most popular state park in Texas for camping?

Garner State Park on the Frio River is among the most popular, especially in summer.

Can I build a campfire in Texas state parks?

Rules vary by park and conditions. Many allow fires only in provided rings and grills, and burn bans may apply. Check with the park.

When is the best time to camp in Texas?

Spring and fall are prime across most of the state. Summer favors rivers and higher elevations.
