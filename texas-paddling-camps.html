Paddling and Camping in Texas: Rivers, Lakes and Bays

An overview of popular Texas paddling destinations for campers, from Hill Country rivers to East Texas bayous and coastal bays.

Texas has a wealth of paddling: spring-fed rivers, cypress swamps, reservoirs and coastal bays. Many of the best places sit next to state parks and campgrounds, which makes it easy to combine a paddle with a night outside. Texas Parks and Wildlife maintains a network of paddling trails around the state. Always check water levels, access points and current rules before you go.

Hill Country rivers

Guadalupe River

One of the most famous paddling and tubing rivers in the state, flowing through cypress-lined stretches in the Hill Country. Guadalupe River State Park offers camping and river access.

Frio River

Clear, cool water through limestone canyons, with Garner State Park at its heart. Summer tubing is a tradition.

San Marcos River

Spring-fed, clear and fairly consistent in temperature year-round.

Llano River

Granite and limestone terrain with rapids and pools. South Llano River State Park is a popular camping base.

East Texas bayous and lakes

Caddo Lake

A labyrinth of cypress-lined bayous straddling the Texas-Louisiana border. Caddo Lake State Park provides camping and access to paddling trails. Navigation can be tricky; take a map.

Big Thicket region

The Big Thicket National Preserve has bayous and rivers in a biologically diverse corner of Southeast Texas.

Coastal paddling

Mustang Island

The paddling trails on the bay side of Mustang Island explore shallow flats and seagrass.

Galveston Island State Park

Paddling trails through marshes and bayous on the bay side of the island.

West Texas

Rio Grande in Big Bend

Multi-day river trips through dramatic canyons. These require planning, permits and experience, or a licensed outfitter.

Water level and safety

Texas rivers can swing from too low to paddle to flood stage. Check flow gauges and park updates. Never paddle a river in flood. Wear PFDs at all times.

Cooking at paddle camps

River camps and sandbar sites are classic spots for grilled fish, sausage, and campfire breakfasts. Check fire rules first; many river corridors have special restrictions, and burn bans are common in Texas summers.

When fires are permitted, a raised steel grill keeps your coals contained, off the sand and gravel, and away from your gear. You know what you are cooking on, rather than whatever is left in an old fire ring on a gravel bar. Flat-pack grills slide into canoes and kayak hatches.

Respecting private land

In Texas, much land along rivers is private. Stay within designated public areas, launch from public access points, and do not trespass on banks.

Pack it out

Rivers carry trash downstream. Bring a mesh bag to pick up litter as you paddle. Pack out everything, including foil, cans and fishing line.

Planning a Texas paddle camp

  1. Pick the water: river, lake, bayou or bay.
  2. Check conditions: flow levels, wind and weather.
  3. Choose access points: public launches and take-outs.
  4. Arrange a shuttle for river trips.
  5. Pick a campground near the water.
  6. Check fire rules and burn bans.

Seasons for paddling in Texas

  • Spring: good flows on many rivers, mild temps.
  • Summer: hot; Hill Country spring-fed rivers stay cool.
  • Fall: comfortable temperatures; rivers may run low.
  • Winter: mild days on the coast and in South Texas.

Paddle-in packing tips for Texas heat

  • Freeze water bottles to act as ice and drinking water.
  • Use a well-insulated cooler, shaded under a wet towel.
  • Wear a sun shirt and hat.
  • Plan for shade breaks.

Cooking on Texas rivers

  • Fresh catch: where legal and kept properly, grill fish the same day.
  • Sausage and peppers: a Texas river classic.
  • Fajitas: prep at home and grill at camp.
  • Breakfast tacos: perfect before a morning paddle.

Safety

  • Wear a PFD.
  • Watch for low-water crossings and dams.
  • Don't paddle in flood conditions.
  • Know your limits.

Etiquette

  • Respect private property along rivers.
  • Keep noise low.
  • Pack out all trash.
  • Leave sandbars and campsites clean.

Why a flat-pack grill suits paddle trips

Flat gear packs best in boats. A flat-pack grill fits in a kayak hatch or along the bottom of a canoe, and on shore it gives you a raised, contained fire, keeping coals off sand and gravel.

Reading Texas rivers before you go

Texas rivers change dramatically with rainfall. A creek that is a lazy float in a normal spring can be a dangerous torrent after a storm upstream, and a summer river can drop so low you drag your boat over gravel. Before any trip:

  • Check river gauges published online for flow and level, and learn what normal looks like for your stretch.
  • Call a local outfitter if there is one. They know current conditions better than anyone.
  • Watch the forecast upstream, not just where you are paddling. Hill Country flash floods can arrive under clear skies.
  • Have a bail-out plan with known take-outs along the route.

Planning a river camping trip

  1. Pick a stretch that suits your group's skill, with a realistic daily distance. Beginners often plan too far.
  2. Arrange a shuttle so a vehicle waits at the take-out.
  3. Confirm where camping is allowed. Much riverside land in Texas is private; camp only at designated sites, public land or with permission.
  4. Pack in dry bags and tie everything into the boat.
  5. File a float plan with someone at home: route, times and when to call for help.

Paddling safety essentials

  • Everyone wears a properly fitted life jacket on the water, the whole time.
  • Scout rapids and low-water crossings you cannot see through, and portage when unsure.
  • Watch for strainers: fallen trees where water flows through but boats and bodies do not.
  • Avoid low-head dams entirely. They create recirculating currents that trap swimmers.
  • Carry a throw rope, whistle and a basic repair kit.
  • Wear sun protection; reflected glare doubles exposure.

Heat management on the water

Summer paddling in Texas can be brutally hot. Start at dawn, take long shade breaks through midday, and drink before you are thirsty. Soak a bandana or hat in the river to cool off. Watch companions for headache, confusion or stopping sweating, which signal heat illness and need immediate cooling.

Cooking on a river trip

Gravel bars make excellent kitchens when camping there is permitted, because they are open, durable and clear of vegetation. Set a raised grill on bare gravel well above the waterline, keep water at hand, and check local rules before lighting anything. River trips reward simple menus:

  • Night one: pre-marinated fajita meat with tortillas.
  • Night two: foil-pack sausage and vegetables.
  • Breakfasts: griddle eggs and tortillas, or oatmeal.
  • Lunches: wraps, jerky, fruit and trail mix that need no cooking.

Packing the boat

  • Heavy items low and centered.
  • Grill and griddle flat at the bottom of the boat, wrapped to protect the hull.
  • Cooler wedged in and tied down.
  • Drinking water and snacks reachable.
  • Rain gear on top.

Leaving river camps clean

Pack out all trash, including food scraps. Scatter strained dishwater well away from the river, never into it. Pack out ash when there are no fire rings, and leave the gravel bar looking untouched for the next paddlers.

Wildlife on Texas waterways

Texas rivers and lakes are rich with wildlife. Herons, egrets and kingfishers hunt along banks, turtles sun on logs, and in some southern and eastern waters you may see alligators. Give all wildlife plenty of room, never feed animals, and keep food sealed in the boat and at camp. Snakes are common near water in warm months; watch where you step and reach on banks and gravel bars, and leave them alone if you see one.

Cooling off safely

Swimming is one of the joys of a summer paddling trip, but rivers hide hazards. Enter feet-first, never dive into unknown water, watch for current, and keep life jackets on kids in and near the water.

After the trip

Rinse boats, paddles and dry bags with fresh water, especially after brackish or muddy rivers, and let everything dry fully before storage. Clean the grill, dry it over a short charcoal fire or in the sun, and wipe on a thin coat of Seasoning Balm. Note river levels and conditions from your trip in a logbook so you can plan the next float with better information.

Questions campers ask

What are the best rivers to paddle in Texas?

The Guadalupe, Frio, San Marcos and Llano are popular Hill Country choices; Caddo Lake is famous in East Texas.

Are there paddling trails in Texas?

Texas Parks and Wildlife maintains a network of paddling trails around the state.

Can I camp on Texas riverbanks?

Much riverbank land is private. Use designated public campsites and access points.
