Camping in the Southeast and Appalachia

Smoky Mountain forests, Blue Ridge overlooks, Appalachian Trail country and Southern rivers: a regional guide for campers.

The southern Appalachians are one of the most beloved camping regions in the United States. Ancient, rounded mountains, deep hardwood forests, waterfalls and some of the greatest biological diversity in the temperate world make the region rewarding in every season. For Texas campers, it is a long drive, but the change from prairie and desert to misty ridges is worth it.

Great Smoky Mountains National Park

Straddling the Tennessee and North Carolina border, the Smokies are famous for the blue haze that gives them their name, for old-growth forest, and for an enormous variety of plants and animals, including black bears and synchronous fireflies in some areas in early summer. The park has developed campgrounds and backcountry sites that require permits. It is consistently one of the most visited national parks in the country, so plan ahead and expect busy roads in peak seasons, especially fall.

Blue Ridge Parkway

The Blue Ridge Parkway is a scenic road running along the crest of the Blue Ridge Mountains, connecting Shenandoah National Park in Virginia with Great Smoky Mountains National Park. Overlooks, short trails and campgrounds along the way make it a classic camping road trip. Speed limits are low and the road is meant to be enjoyed slowly.

Shenandoah National Park

In Virginia, Shenandoah follows the Blue Ridge with Skyline Drive running its length. It is known for overlooks, waterfalls and sections of the Appalachian Trail.

The Appalachian Trail

The Appalachian Trail runs roughly from Springer Mountain in Georgia to Mount Katahdin in Maine. You do not need to be a thru-hiker to enjoy it. Many campers base at a campground and day-hike a section, or do a short overnight on the trail.

National forests of the southern mountains

The region has large national forests, such as the Pisgah and Nantahala in North Carolina, the Chattahoochee in Georgia, and the Cherokee in Tennessee. They offer developed campgrounds, dispersed camping where permitted, rivers and waterfalls.

Rivers and paddling

Southern Appalachian rivers range from gentle floats to serious whitewater. Rafting outfitters operate on several famous rivers. If you paddle on your own, match the river to your skills, check levels and wear a PFD.

Southern coastal and lowland highlights

Beyond the mountains, the Southeast offers swamps, springs and beaches:

  • Okefenokee Swamp in southeast Georgia, a vast blackwater wetland with paddling trails.
  • Florida's freshwater springs, many protected in state parks, known for clear water.
  • Congaree National Park in South Carolina, known for old-growth bottomland hardwood forest.

When to go

  • Spring: wildflowers and waterfalls at their best. Weather is changeable.
  • Summer: green and lush, warm in the valleys, cooler at elevation. Afternoon thunderstorms are common.
  • Fall: spectacular color and crowds to match.
  • Winter: quiet; some high roads and campgrounds close, and snow and ice are possible at elevation.

Bear country habits

Black bears live throughout the southern Appalachians. Follow food storage rules strictly:

  1. Store food, coolers, trash and scented items in a hard-sided vehicle or bear-resistant locker as required.
  2. Never leave food unattended at a picnic table.
  3. Cook and eat away from your tent.
  4. Clean the grill and pack out grease and scraps.
  5. Never feed bears or other wildlife.

A clean, packable grill helps here. A personal grill that you scrape, wipe and stash in the vehicle after dinner leaves far less scent behind than a public fire ring with old grease and food scraps.

Fire rules and firewood

Many southern parks and forests have strict firewood rules to stop the spread of tree pests, often requiring heat-treated or locally sourced firewood. Burn bans happen during drought, especially in fall. Always check current rules.

Humidity and gear care

Southern humidity means gear rarely dries overnight. Bring extra socks, a tarp for a dry kitchen area, and synthetic layers. Raw carbon steel needs extra care in humid air: dry it, oil it and store it in a breathable bag.

Sample week-long road trip

  • Days 1 and 2: campground base in the Smokies; waterfall hikes and a scenic drive.
  • Days 3 and 4: Blue Ridge Parkway campgrounds; overlooks and short trails.
  • Days 5 and 6: national forest campground near a river; float or swim.
  • Day 7: drive home, with a trailhead cookout on the way.

Camp menu for mountain nights

  • Grilled trout with lemon and herbs
  • Skillet cornbread
  • Smoked sausage with peppers and onions
  • Apple cobbler in cast iron
  • Hot cocoa heated on the edge of the grill

Leave No Trace in popular parks

Popular parks see heavy use. Stick to trails, avoid shortcutting switchbacks, pack out all trash, and leave wildflowers for others to enjoy.

Humidity and your gear

The Southeast is humid much of the year, and that shapes how you camp. Tents collect heavy condensation overnight, towels never quite dry, and bare steel rusts faster than in the arid West.

  • Ventilate the tent by opening vents and pitching the fly taut so air moves between layers.
  • Hang wet gear under a tarp in a breeze rather than stuffing it in the car.
  • Bring extra socks and keep one pair sealed in a dry bag for sleeping.
  • Oil the grill after every cook and dry it over the last of the coals before packing. A thin coat of Seasoning Balm keeps humid air off the steel.

Rain is part of the plan

Afternoon showers are common in mountain summers. Set a tarp high over the kitchen and seating area as soon as you arrive. Keep the grill out from under it, in the open, so heat and smoke rise freely. Pack a few meals that cook fast, so you can get supper done in a dry window between storms.

Ticks, chiggers and mosquitoes

  • Treat clothing with a permethrin product at home following label directions, and use repellent on skin.
  • Tuck pants into socks on overgrown trails.
  • Do a full tick check every evening, including hairline, behind ears and behind knees.
  • Remove attached ticks promptly with fine tweezers, pulling straight out.

Bears in the southern mountains

Black bears live throughout the southern Appalachians. Many campgrounds there require food storage in vehicles or bear-resistant lockers, and some backcountry areas require bear canisters or hangs. Never leave a cooler out unattended, cook and eat away from the tent, and clean the grill right after use so grease does not draw visitors overnight. Bag scraps with the trash and store it like food.

Mountain driving

Southern mountain roads are narrow and twisting, with steep grades. Use lower gears on long descents to save your brakes, watch for cyclists and wildlife, and allow more time than the map suggests. Trailers and big RVs should check road restrictions before committing to a scenic route.

Firewood rules

Many southeastern parks restrict importing firewood to slow the spread of tree-killing insects. Buy local or certified heat-treated wood near the campground, or skip wood and burn charcoal in your own grill where permitted. Never carry leftover wood home or to another park.

Seasonal highlights

  • Spring: wildflowers and waterfalls at full flow; nights can still be cold up high.
  • Summer: green and humid, cooler at elevation, with afternoon storms.
  • Fall: the most popular season for leaf color, so book early.
  • Winter: quiet campgrounds at lower elevations, with some high roads closing for ice.

Cooking in a humid camp

Charcoal lights more slowly when damp. Keep it in a sealed bin or heavy bag, and use a chimney starter to get it going. Mountain trout, local sausage, sweet corn and peaches in summer make easy southern camp meals on the grate.

Waterfall safety

Southern mountains are full of waterfalls, but wet rocks around them are extremely slippery. Stay on trails and behind railings, never climb on falls, and keep kids close. Many serious injuries happen at the top of falls, where current and slick rock catch people off guard.

Mountain camp breakfast

Cool mountain mornings call for hot food. A skillet of country ham or sausage, eggs and griddle biscuits fuels a day of hiking.

A final note

Book fall leaf-season sites as early as possible.

Questions campers ask

What is the best time to camp in the Smokies?

Spring for wildflowers and fall for color are popular; summer is lush and busy, and winter is quiet.

Do I need a permit for backcountry camping in the Smokies?

Backcountry camping in Great Smoky Mountains National Park requires a permit.

Are there bears in the southern Appalachians?

Yes. Black bears live throughout the region; store food properly and never feed wildlife.
