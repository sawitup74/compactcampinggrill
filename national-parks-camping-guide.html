Camping in U.S. National Parks: Planning and Favorite Parks

How national park camping works, how to plan around reservations and crowds, and what famous parks are known for.

National parks hold some of the most spectacular camping in the world. They also attract enormous crowds, have specific rules, and often require planning far in advance. Here is how camping in national parks works and what some of the most beloved parks offer.

How national park camping works

  • Developed campgrounds with roads, restrooms and often water. Many take reservations through the federal reservation system, while some still offer first-come, first-served sites.
  • Backcountry camping usually requires a permit.
  • Group sites are available in many parks.
  • Nearby national forests and state parks are good backup options.

Planning tips

  • Reservations for popular parks can open months ahead and fill quickly.
  • Arrive early for first-come sites.
  • Check road and campground opening dates; many mountain campgrounds are seasonal.
  • Follow food storage rules, which are strict in bear country.
  • Check fire restrictions, which may change daily.

Texas national parks

Big Bend National Park

Desert, mountains and river along the Rio Grande in far West Texas. The Chisos Mountains rise in the middle of the park, and the park is famous for some of the darkest night skies in the country.

Guadalupe Mountains National Park

Home to Guadalupe Peak, the highest point in Texas, plus McKittrick Canyon, known for fall color.

Favorites across the country

Great Smoky Mountains

Straddling Tennessee and North Carolina, this is famously one of the most visited national parks, with misty mountains, old-growth forests and abundant wildlife.

Grand Canyon

The South Rim is open year-round while the North Rim is seasonal. Camping along the rim offers unforgettable sunsets.

Yellowstone

The first national park, known for geysers including Old Faithful, hot springs and wildlife such as bison, elk and bears.

Yosemite

Granite cliffs, waterfalls and giant sequoias in California's Sierra Nevada.

Zion

Red sandstone canyons in Utah, famous for hikes along the Virgin River.

Rocky Mountain

High peaks, alpine tundra and elk in Colorado.

Acadia

Rocky Atlantic coast and granite mountains on the coast of Maine.

Olympic

Rainforest, mountains and wild coastline in Washington.

Food storage and wildlife

Many parks require food, coolers and scented items to be stored in bear lockers or hard-sided vehicles. Never feed wildlife. Keep a clean camp. A clean, packable grill that you store with your food and scrub after use helps keep scents down.

Fire rules in national parks

Fires are usually permitted only in established rings or grills in developed campgrounds, and they may be banned during high fire danger. Some parks allow portable grills and stoves; others restrict them. Always confirm current regulations.

National park road trips often mean long drives, quick setups and changing campgrounds every night or two. Reservation strategy

  • Know when reservations open for your target park.
  • Be online the moment they open for popular dates.
  • Have backup dates and parks.
  • Check for cancellations regularly.
  • Consider weekdays and shoulder seasons.

Choosing a campground inside a park

  • Location: near the trails or sights you most want to see.
  • Elevation: affects temperature.
  • Amenities: water, restrooms, showers (not always available).
  • RV length limits.

Outside-the-park options

National forests, state parks and private campgrounds near national parks are often easier to book and less crowded.

Making the most of your visit

  • Get up early to beat crowds.
  • Visit lesser-known areas of popular parks.
  • Attend ranger programs.
  • Join a junior ranger program with kids.

Park passes

Many national parks charge entrance fees. An annual pass that covers federal recreation lands can be worthwhile if you visit several parks. Check current options.

Wildlife safety

Keep a safe distance from all wildlife. Use binoculars and zoom lenses. Never approach or feed animals.

Cooking in national park campgrounds

Many campgrounds have fire rings and grills, which see heavy use. Your own grill guarantees a clean surface. Follow fire restrictions and food storage rules.

Big Bend and Guadalupe Mountains

Combine the two Texas national parks in a West Texas road trip.

Grand Canyon, Zion and other parks in the region on a two-week trip.

Rockies summer

Rocky Mountain National Park, then north to Grand Teton and Yellowstone.

Respect the parks

National parks protect fragile places for future generations. Follow rules, stay on trails and leave no trace.

Reservation strategies

Popular national park campgrounds often fill quickly. Many sites are reservable in advance through the federal recreation reservation system, and some parks open booking windows months ahead. Strategies:

  • Know when your target campground's booking window opens.
  • Be flexible with dates and campgrounds.
  • Check for cancellations close to your trip.
  • Look at nearby national forest and state park campgrounds as backups.

Arriving at the park

Stop at the visitor center for maps, current conditions, fire restrictions and ranger programs. Rangers can point you to quieter trails and current wildlife activity.

Campground etiquette

  • Respect quiet hours.
  • Keep your site tidy.
  • Don't walk through other sites.
  • Keep pets leashed and under control.
  • Keep lights low at night.

Food storage in parks

Many parks require food to be stored in lockers or vehicles. Bear country parks have strict rules: never leave food, coolers or scented items unattended.

Fires in national parks

Fire rules vary by park and season. Many parks only allow fires in designated rings or grills, and restrictions can be imposed during dry conditions. Firewood often must be local or certified to prevent the spread of pests. Check current rules on arrival and follow them.

Ranger programs

Many parks offer evening programs, guided walks and junior ranger activities. These are excellent for families.

Wildlife watching

Keep safe distances. Use binoculars and telephoto lenses, and never feed wildlife.

Leave No Trace in parks

Stay on trails, pack out trash, and leave natural and cultural objects in place.

Weather and elevation

Parks can span huge elevation ranges. Pack layers and check weather for each area you will visit.

Planning a multi-park road trip

Many campers string several national parks together in one trip. A few habits make these trips smoother:

  1. Reserve the anchor nights first, usually the most popular park, then fill in around them.
  2. Keep driving days short. Distances in the West are long, and arriving after dark at an unfamiliar campground is stressful.
  3. Build in a rest day every few days for laundry, groceries and recharging.
  4. Check each park's current alerts for road closures, construction and fire restrictions.
  5. Carry an annual pass if you'll visit several federal sites; check current options on the official site.

First-come, first-served campgrounds

Some parks keep campgrounds or loops that don't take reservations. These fill early in busy seasons, often by morning. Arrive early, have backups nearby, and follow the posted process for claiming a site.

Cooking in national park campgrounds

Most developed campgrounds provide a picnic table and a fire ring or grill at each site. Many campers prefer bringing their own grill for a clean surface. Where charcoal is allowed, set your grill on the site's gravel pad or in the area the park designates, follow any fire restrictions, and keep food secured between meals. Never leave a cooler on the table when you walk away.

Altitude and desert parks

Parks span sea-level coastlines, high mountains and hot deserts. Pack layers, sun protection and extra water, and plan hikes for cooler times of day in desert parks.

Respecting closures

Areas close for wildlife, safety or restoration. Follow all closure signs; they protect both you and the park.

Visiting in the shoulder season

Spring and fall shoulder seasons often bring thinner crowds, easier reservations and milder weather at many parks. Some services and roads may still be closed early or late in the year, so check each park's current conditions page before you go. Pack for a wide range of temperatures, since shoulder-season nights can turn cold quickly, especially at elevation. A warm supper cooked on your own grill, where charcoal is allowed, makes those crisp evenings at camp something to look forward to rather than endure.

Questions campers ask

Do I need a reservation to camp in a national park?

Many campgrounds require reservations, especially in peak season. Some still offer first-come sites.

Can I bring my own grill to a national park campground?

Many allow it, but rules vary and fire restrictions can apply. Check the park's current regulations.

What is the highest point in Texas?

Guadalupe Peak in Guadalupe Mountains National Park.
