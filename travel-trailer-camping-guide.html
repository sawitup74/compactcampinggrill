Travel Trailer and RV Camping: A First-Timer's Guide

Choosing a rig, hookups, campground etiquette and cooking outside your trailer.

An RV or travel trailer brings home comforts to the campground: a real bed, a fridge, a roof that does not drip. It also brings new skills to learn, from towing to hookups. This guide covers what first-timers need to know.

Types of rigs

  • Travel trailers are towed behind a truck or SUV. A wide range of sizes.
  • Fifth wheels hitch in a truck bed and offer roomy floor plans.
  • Pop-up and hybrid campers fold down for towing and set up with canvas or soft walls.
  • Motorhomes (Class A, B and C) are drivable RVs, from van conversions to bus-sized coaches.
  • Truck campers ride in a pickup bed.

Hookups explained

  • Full hookup: water, electric and sewer.
  • Partial hookup: usually water and electric.
  • Dry camping: no hookups; you rely on tanks and batteries.

Learn your rig's electrical needs (often 30 or 50 amp), bring the right adapters, and use a water pressure regulator and drinking-water hose.

Before your first trip

  • Practice towing and backing in an empty parking lot.
  • Check tire pressure and lug nuts.
  • Test lights and brakes.
  • Learn to level the rig.
  • Make a departure checklist: steps up, antenna down, awning in, hitch secured, chains crossed, breakaway cable connected.

Campground etiquette for RVers

  • Keep generators within quiet hours.
  • Keep outdoor lights low.
  • Don't let sewer hoses leak.
  • Stay within your site.

Cooking outside the RV

Most RVs have a kitchen inside, but cooking outdoors keeps the rig cool, keeps grease and smell out, and is half the fun of camping.

The usual outdoor RV choices include a bulky grill strapped to the back bumper, which takes up storage and rattles over every bump, or cheap folding grills that rust in a season. A flat-pack steel grill makes more sense for RV storage. The Slot Grill Compact packs to about an inch thick, small enough to slide in an RV closet or pass-through bay. The Full Size packs to about an inch and a quarter for families.

Fire and RV safety

  • Set the grill on a stable, non-combustible surface, well away from awnings, slides and the rig itself.
  • Never cook under the awning.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher in the RV and know how to use it.
  • Never use a charcoal grill inside the RV or a screened enclosure.

Where to camp

State parks, national parks, national forests, private RV resorts, and public land all welcome RVs. Check length limits; many older campgrounds have sites too short for large rigs.

Living small

Keep the RV organized with bins, use hooks and command strips, and store outdoor gear in exterior bays. A flat-pack grill and a bag of charcoal in a sealed bin is all you need for outdoor cooking.

Towing basics

  • Know your tow vehicle's towing capacity and payload.
  • Use the right hitch, weight distribution and sway control as needed.
  • Check tire pressure on both vehicle and trailer.
  • Practice turning, backing and parking in an empty lot.
  • Leave extra following distance; braking takes longer.
  • Plan routes with fuel stops that can accommodate a trailer.

Backing into a site

  1. Pull past the site and stop.
  2. Have a spotter outside with a clear view.
  3. Agree on hand signals or use radios.
  4. Put your hand at the bottom of the steering wheel: move your hand in the direction you want the trailer to go.
  5. Make small corrections.
  6. Get out and look if unsure.

Setup checklist

  1. Level the trailer side to side with blocks.
  2. Chock the wheels.
  3. Unhitch.
  4. Level front to back with the jack.
  5. Lower stabilizers.
  6. Connect electric.
  7. Connect water with a pressure regulator.
  8. Connect sewer, if available.
  9. Extend slides and awning.
  10. Set up the outdoor kitchen well away from the trailer.

Teardown checklist

  1. Pack outdoor gear, including the grill once it is completely cold.
  2. Retract awning and slides.
  3. Disconnect sewer, water and electric.
  4. Raise stabilizers.
  5. Hitch and connect chains, breakaway cable and lights.
  6. Test lights and brakes.
  7. Remove wheel chocks.
  8. Walk around once more.

Tank management

  • Empty black tanks when at least half full, followed by gray tanks to rinse the hose.
  • Use RV-safe toilet paper.
  • Wear gloves.

Power

Know your rig's amperage and avoid running too many high-draw appliances at once. Use a surge protector.

Boondocking

Camping without hookups means conserving water and power, and managing tanks. Solar panels and batteries help.

Outdoor living

An outdoor rug, chairs, a table, and a grill turn your site into a living space. A flat-pack grill stores easily and gives you a clean cooking surface, rather than the pedestal grill that every RV in the park used before you.

Before the first tow

  1. Know your numbers. Find your vehicle's towing capacity and the trailer's loaded weight in the owner's manuals, and stay within both.
  2. Inspect the hitch, ball size, safety chains and breakaway cable.
  3. Check tires on both vehicle and trailer, including pressure and age.
  4. Test the lights with a helper: brakes, turn signals and running lights.
  5. Practice in an empty lot, especially backing up and tight turns.

Driving with a trailer

  • Allow much more stopping distance.
  • Take wide turns.
  • Drive slower than usual, especially in wind.
  • Watch for sway; slow down gradually if it starts.
  • Plan fuel stops with room to pull through.

Arriving and setting up

  1. Check in and confirm your site.
  2. Pull or back in with a spotter.
  3. Level the trailer side to side with blocks, then chock the wheels.
  4. Unhitch and level front to back with the tongue jack.
  5. Lower stabilizers.
  6. Connect power, water and sewer as available.
  7. Open slides and set up outdoor gear.

Outdoor kitchen setup at a trailer site

Cooking outside keeps grease, heat and odors out of the trailer. Set your grill on a stable surface away from the trailer, awning and any slide-outs, upwind of the door. A flat-pack grill stores easily in a pass-through bay or under a bed and comes out in about a minute.

Trailer storage tips

  • Keep heavy items low and over the axles.
  • Secure loose items before travel.
  • Use bins in cabinets.
  • Store the grill and griddle flat to save space.

Tank management

Track fresh, gray and black tank levels. Dump at designated stations, rinse the black tank, and always wear gloves.

Leaving the site

  • Close slides and retract stabilizers.
  • Disconnect and stow hoses and cables.
  • Secure doors, vents and cabinets.
  • Hitch up and do a walk-around.
  • Test lights before driving.

Common first-year mistakes

  • Overloading.
  • Forgetting to retract steps or antennas.
  • Leaving vents open in rain.
  • Skipping the walk-around.

Choosing your first travel trailer campground

For your first few trips, pick a campground that makes towing easy. Look for pull-through sites, which let you drive in and out without backing, and paved or gravel loops wide enough for long rigs. Read the site length listed in the reservation system and compare it with your combined trailer and tow vehicle length, leaving room for a slide-out and a picnic table. Call the campground if you're unsure; staff can often recommend sites that work well for first-timers.

Packing a trailer's outdoor gear

Many trailer owners keep a dedicated outdoor kit in the pass-through storage so it's always ready:

  • Folding table and chairs
  • Outdoor rug or mat for the entry
  • Flat-pack grill and griddle
  • Charcoal in a sealed bin
  • Chimney starter and gloves
  • Wash bin and biodegradable soap
  • Lantern or string lights
  • Wheel chocks and leveling blocks

Weight distribution and sway

Uneven weight is the most common cause of trailer sway. Load heavy items low and over or just ahead of the axles, never at the far back. Many towing setups use a weight-distribution hitch with sway control; follow the manufacturer's guidance and have it set up properly by a qualified shop if you're unsure.

Weather awareness

Trailers catch wind like a sail. Avoid driving in high crosswinds, retract awnings when you leave the site or when wind picks up, and keep an eye on severe weather alerts.

Outdoor cooking etiquette at RV parks

RV parks pack sites closer together than many campgrounds. Position the grill so smoke drifts away from neighbors' windows and awnings, keep it on the pad, and clean up promptly after cooking.

One more tip

Keep a simple log of trips, sites and notes to remember favorite spots for next time.

Questions campers ask

What is a full hookup campsite?

A site with water, electric and sewer connections.

Can I use a charcoal grill at an RV campground?

Usually, if fire rules allow. Keep it away from the rig and awnings, and never use it inside.

Where should I store a grill in an RV?

A flat-pack grill fits in a closet or exterior bay.
