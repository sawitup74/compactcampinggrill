Camping Weather: Heat, Storms, Wind and Cold

How to read forecasts, prepare for Texas heat and Gulf storms, and keep camp safe when weather turns.

Weather decides more camping trips than any gear choice. A warm, sunny forecast can still bring an afternoon thunderstorm, and a cold front can drop temperatures dramatically overnight. Learning to read weather and plan for it is the most important skill a camper can build.

Before you go

  • Check the forecast for the specific park, not just the nearest city.
  • Look at hourly forecasts for wind and storm timing.
  • Note overnight lows, not just daytime highs.
  • Check for burn bans, flood watches and heat advisories.

Heat

Heat illness is a serious risk in Texas and across the South.

  • Drink water steadily throughout the day, more than feels necessary.
  • Plan strenuous activities for early morning and evening.
  • Seek shade during midday.
  • Know the signs of heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, dizziness. Move to shade, cool off and hydrate.
  • Heat stroke is a medical emergency: confusion, hot skin, possibly no sweating. Call for help immediately.

For cooking in heat, keep the menu quick. A compact grill heats fast and needs only a small amount of charcoal, which keeps the cook area cooler than a big fire.

Thunderstorms and lightning

  • If you hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.
  • Avoid high ground, open fields and isolated tall trees.
  • A hard-topped vehicle with windows up is safer than a tent.
  • Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back out.

Flash floods

Many beautiful campsites sit near creeks and in low areas. Heavy rain upstream can raise water quickly, even if it is not raining where you are. Never camp in a dry wash. Know your exit route. Never drive through flooded roads.

Wind

Wind collapses tents, sends canopies flying and spreads fire.

  • Stake tents and guy them out fully.
  • Take down canopies in strong gusts.
  • Scale fires down or extinguish them in wind.
  • Charcoal in a raised steel grill is far easier to keep contained than an open wood fire, but put it out when wind gets strong and gusty.

Cold

  • Pack layers and a warm hat.
  • Insulate from the ground with a good pad.
  • Eat warm, calorie-rich meals.
  • Keep sleeping clothes dry.
  • Watch for hypothermia: shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness.

Rain

A good tarp over the kitchen turns rain from a disaster into a cozy afternoon. Pitch it high enough to keep away from the grill's heat and angled so water runs off. Never set up a grill under a tent, canopy or low tarp. Cook in the open with the tarp nearby and well above.

Hurricanes and tropical storms on the Gulf Coast

Coastal camping in late summer and fall means watching the tropics. If a storm is forecast, cancel or leave early. Parks may close. Do not wait it out on a barrier island.

Weather and food safety

Hot weather melts ice fast. Shade the cooler, cover it with a blanket, and drain meltwater as needed. In very hot conditions, plan to cook perishable meat early in the trip.

Weather tools

  • Weather apps: check hourly forecasts and radar.
  • NOAA Weather Radio: broadcasts alerts, useful where cell service is spotty.
  • Park alerts: check before you go.
  • Sky reading: building cumulus clouds in the afternoon signal storms; a sudden drop in temperature and wind shift often come before a storm.

Seasonal guide for Texas camping

  • Spring: severe storms, hail and tornadoes possible, especially in North Texas.
  • Summer: extreme heat, afternoon thunderstorms, possible tropical systems on the coast.
  • Fall: pleasant weather, cold fronts, the tail end of hurricane season.
  • Winter: mild in the south; freezes possible across much of the state.

Severe storm plan

  1. Know where the nearest sturdy shelter is.
  2. Monitor alerts.
  3. Secure or pack loose gear.
  4. Put out fires and grills.
  5. Move to shelter or a hard-topped vehicle.
  6. Wait until the storm passes.

Hail

Hail can damage tents and vehicles. Shelter in a sturdy building or hard-topped vehicle.

Tornadoes

Tents and trailers offer no protection. Know your campground's shelter options.

Fog

Coastal and river fog can reduce visibility for driving and paddling.

Heat index

High humidity makes heat more dangerous. Check the heat index, not just the temperature.

Grilling in different weather

  • Wind: use a windbreak (not flammable), keep the fire small, and put it out in strong gusts.
  • Rain: cook in the open with a tarp high above.
  • Cold: charcoal takes longer to heat up; bring extra fuel.
  • Heat: keep cooking short and stay hydrated.

Packing for weather

  • Rain gear
  • Warm layers
  • Sun hat and sunscreen
  • Extra tarp and cord
  • Extra stakes
  • Waterproof bags

Reading the forecast like a camper

A city forecast is not a campsite forecast. When you plan a trip, look beyond the headline temperature.

  • Overnight low: this decides your sleeping bag and layers, and it is often colder at camp than in the nearest town, especially in valleys and at higher elevation.
  • Wind speed and gusts: gusts matter more than averages for tents, tarps and fire safety.
  • Chance and timing of rain: a 30 percent chance in the afternoon means something very different from all-day rain.
  • Dew point: high dew points mean muggy nights and heavy condensation in tents.
  • Hazard alerts: red flag warnings, flood watches, heat advisories and winter storm warnings.

Thunderstorm safety

  • When thunder roars, go indoors. A hard-topped vehicle with windows up is safer than a tent.
  • Avoid open fields, ridgelines, lone trees and water during storms.
  • Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before going back out.
  • Don't camp under dead limbs, which can fall in wind.

Flash floods

Never camp in dry washes or low spots near creeks when rain is possible anywhere upstream. Never drive through flooded roads; it takes surprisingly little moving water to sweep a vehicle away.

Heat

  • Plan activities for mornings and evenings.
  • Set up shade over camp.
  • Drink water steadily and eat salty snacks.
  • Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke.
  • Cook meals that need short grilling times so you are not standing over heat at midday.

Cold

  • Bring a sleeping bag rated below the expected low.
  • Insulate from the ground with a good pad.
  • Eat a hot supper before bed.
  • Stay dry; wet clothes lose insulation.
  • Keep a hat on at night.

Wind

  • Stake and guy tents fully.
  • Lower tarps and canopies or take them down in gusts.
  • Skip fires when winds are strong, and always follow red flag warnings.
  • Place the grill out of the wind, behind a vehicle, on bare ground away from everything flammable.

Rain

  • Pitch the tent on high ground.
  • Use a tarp over the kitchen area, set high and well clear of the grill.
  • Keep a dry bag of clothes.
  • Ventilate the tent to reduce condensation.

Weather and cooking

Charcoal burns hotter and faster in wind and slower in cold, damp air. Adjust the amount of fuel and cooking times. In rain, cook under the open sky with the tarp high above and well to the side, never under it directly.

Weather tools for camp

Download a reliable weather app with radar before you lose signal, and consider a small weather radio for areas without coverage. Learn to read the sky: building towers of clouds in the afternoon often mean storms, a sudden drop in temperature and gusty wind can signal a storm's arrival, and a ring around the moon can hint at moisture moving in.

Cold fronts

Cold fronts can drop temperatures quickly and bring strong winds. When one is forecast, stake down gear, secure the tarp, and have warm layers ready.

Fog and dew

Mornings near water often bring thick fog and heavy dew. Store gear in the car or under a tarp overnight, cover chairs, and keep charcoal sealed so it lights easily in the morning. Wipe the grill dry and give it a quick coat of oil if it got wet overnight.

One more tip

After any big storm, check the campsite for downed branches, standing water and loosened stakes before letting kids roam.

A final note

Check the forecast every morning at camp.

Questions campers ask

Is a tent safe in a lightning storm?

No. A hard-topped vehicle is safer. Avoid high ground and isolated trees.

Can I grill in the rain at camp?

Yes, in the open with a tarp well above. Never under a tent or low canopy.

How do I keep my cooler cold in Texas heat?

Pre-chill, use block ice, keep it shaded and covered, and open it as little as possible.
