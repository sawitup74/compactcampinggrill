Campfire Safety: The Complete Camper's Guide

How to choose a spot, build a fire that behaves, keep kids safe, and put it out cold every single time.

A campfire is the oldest piece of camping technology, and it remains the one most likely to go wrong. Most wildfires that start from campsites begin with a fire someone thought was out, or a spark that drifted farther than expected. The good news is that safe fire habits are simple and repeatable. Learn them once, practice them every trip, and teach them to anyone who camps with you.

Step one: know the rules before you pack

Fire rules depend on where you are and what time of year it is. A state park might allow fires only in established rings or raised containers. A national forest might allow dispersed fires most of the year and ban them during drought. Some areas allow charcoal and gas but not wood. Rules can change overnight when conditions turn dry or windy.

  • Check the managing agency's current alerts before leaving home.
  • Ask at the entrance station or campground host when you arrive.
  • Read the posted signs at your site.
  • If you are unsure, do not light it.

Step two: pick the right spot

A good fire location is boring on purpose. You want nothing nearby that can catch.

  • At least 15 feet from tents, vehicles, awnings and trees is a common rule of thumb, more in wind.
  • No overhanging branches above the fire.
  • Bare mineral soil, gravel or a non-combustible pad under the fire.
  • Downwind of where people sit, so smoke drifts away and sparks don't land on gear.

If you are using a raised steel grill as your fire container, set it on a stable, level, non-combustible surface. Raised designs keep the coals off the ground, but the ground under and around still needs to be clear.

Step three: build it to behave

Big fires are not better fires. A small, hot fire is easier to manage, makes better coals for cooking, and produces less smoke.

Fuel ladder

Gather three sizes: tinder (dry grass, bark shavings, a commercial fire starter), kindling (pencil to thumb-thick sticks) and fuel wood (wrist-thick splits). Light the tinder, feed kindling as it catches, then add fuel wood gradually. Never use gasoline or other accelerants to start or revive a fire.

Firewood rules

Many parks ask you to buy firewood locally or use certified wood, because moving firewood can spread tree-killing insects and diseases. Do not cut live trees or break branches off standing ones. Dead and down wood collection may be prohibited in some parks. Charcoal sidesteps much of this and gives a steadier cooking heat.

Step four: supervise like a lifeguard

Someone responsible must be at the fire the entire time it burns. That includes during dinner cleanup, bathroom trips, and that last stretch when everyone is sleepy.

  • Keep a full bucket or jug of water and a shovel next to the fire.
  • Set a "fire circle" boundary for kids, a few big steps from the edge.
  • Teach kids to walk, never run, near the fire.
  • No horseplay, no throwing things into the fire.
  • Roasting sticks should be long, and hot tips should be pointed at the ground when walking.

Step five: put it out cold

"Dead out" means cool to the touch. Here is the method rangers teach everywhere: drown, stir, feel.

  1. Let the fire burn down to ash where possible.
  2. Pour water slowly over all embers, not just the red ones.
  3. Stir the ash and coals with a stick or shovel, exposing hidden embers.
  4. Pour more water and stir again.
  5. Hold the back of your hand near the ash. If you feel heat, repeat.

Dirt alone is not a reliable extinguisher. It can insulate coals and let them smolder for hours. And while we are on the subject, please use water from a jug. The campers who use the ring tomorrow will thank you, even if they never know why.

Burns and first aid basics

Pack a small first aid kit with burn gel and sterile dressings. For minor burns, cool the area under clean, cool water for several minutes, remove rings or tight items nearby, and cover loosely. Seek medical help for large, deep or facial burns, or burns on small children.

Wind and weather

Wind turns sparks into embers that travel. If gusts pick up, scale your fire down or put it out. Many burn restrictions are triggered by low humidity and high wind together. A compact charcoal setup in a steel grill is far easier to control in breezy conditions than an open wood fire, but even that should be extinguished when gusts make things unpredictable.

Cooking fires versus warming fires

A cooking fire wants a bed of even coals. A warming fire wants flame. Trying to cook over a roaring bonfire burns the outside of food and leaves the inside raw. That is a big reason many campers separate the two: cook on a charcoal grill first, then add a little wood afterward for a short evening fire, if allowed. Using one steel container for both keeps everything compact and contained.

Leave the site cleaner than you found it

Pack out foil, food scraps and anything that did not burn. Never bury trash or coals. If the campground provides an ash bin, use it once the ash is cold. If not, pack cold ash home in a metal can with a lid.

Campfire safety checklist

  • Check rules and burn bans.
  • Choose a safe location.
  • Keep water and a shovel nearby.
  • Build a small fire.
  • Never leave it unattended.
  • Put it out cold.

What not to burn

A campfire is not a trash can. Never burn:

  • Plastic bottles, wrappers or foam cups
  • Cans and glass, which don't burn and leave sharp debris
  • Treated, painted or pressure-treated lumber
  • Food scraps, which attract animals and rarely burn completely
  • Green or live branches
  • Aerosol cans or batteries, which can explode

Burning trash releases toxic smoke into your neighbors' sites and leaves a mess for the next campers. Keep a trash bag handy and pack it out instead.

Wind and embers

Even a small fire can throw embers on a gusty day. If wind is strong enough to blow sparks beyond the ring or fire pit, let the fire die down or skip it altogether. Watch the forecast for red flag warnings, which signal dangerous fire weather.

Fire tools to keep nearby

  • A full bucket or jug of water
  • A shovel or long stick for stirring ashes
  • Heat-resistant gloves
  • A fire extinguisher in your vehicle

Burns first aid

For minor burns, cool the area under cool running water for several minutes, then cover with a clean, dry dressing. Don't use ice or butter. Seek medical care for large, deep or blistering burns, or burns on the face, hands or joints.

A contained alternative

Public fire rings vary in condition, and many are full of old trash and ash. A raised steel fire pit you bring yourself keeps the fire up off the ground, contained within solid walls, and easy to put out completely.

Teaching fire safety to kids

Show kids the safe zone around the fire, how to roast with a long stick while standing back, and what to do if clothing catches fire: stop, drop and roll.

One more tip

Before bed or leaving camp, hold your hand just above the ashes. If you feel any heat, add more water and stir again until everything is cold.

Questions campers ask

How far should a campfire be from my tent?

A common guideline is at least 15 feet from tents, vehicles and trees, and farther in windy conditions. Follow any distance posted by your campground.

Can I put out a campfire with dirt?

Dirt can trap heat and let coals smolder. Use plenty of water, stir and feel for heat until everything is cool.

Why can't I bring firewood from home?

Moving firewood can spread invasive insects and tree diseases. Many parks require local or certified firewood.
