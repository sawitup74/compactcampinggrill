Camping in the Ozarks and Arkansas

A regional highlight for Texas campers: the Buffalo National River, Hot Springs National Park and Ozark forests.

For Texas campers looking for a change of scenery within driving distance, Arkansas offers forested mountains, clear rivers and caves.

Buffalo National River

The first national river in the United States, flowing freely through the Ozarks with bluffs and gravel bars. Canoeing, kayaking and gravel bar camping are popular.

Hot Springs National Park

Known for its historic bathhouses and thermal springs, with a campground and trails.

Planning a float trip

Ozark rivers are famous for float trips by canoe or kayak, often with overnight gravel bar camps. Water levels vary greatly by season and rainfall: spring often has the most water, while late summer can be too low for some stretches. Check current river levels and conditions with the managing agency or a local outfitter before you go.

Float trip checklist

  • PFD for every paddler
  • Dry bags for all gear
  • Map of river access points
  • Water treatment
  • Sun protection
  • Shoes that can get wet
  • Trash bag to carry out everything

Gravel bar camping tips

  • Camp high on the bar, well above the water line, in case the river rises overnight.
  • Watch the weather upstream; rain miles away can raise the river.
  • Gravel can be lumpy; bring a good sleeping pad.
  • Keep fires small, contained and away from vegetation, and only where rules allow.
  • Leave no fire scars; pack out cold ash.

Hiking highlights

The Ozarks are known for waterfalls, bluffs and hollows. Spring rains bring waterfalls to life; fall brings color. Trails can be steep and rocky; wear sturdy shoes.

Hot Springs and the Ouachitas

Hot Springs National Park is unusual among national parks for its historic downtown bathhouse district. Nearby, the Ouachita Mountains run east and west, unlike most American ranges, and offer forests, lakes and long trails.

Weather

Summers are hot and humid. Winters can bring cold snaps and occasional ice. Spring storms can be severe; monitor forecasts and know your shelter options.

Cooking at Ozark camps

Many float campers cook on gravel bars. A raised grill keeps coals off the gravel and contains the fire. Bring charcoal rather than gathering wood, which may be restricted or scarce on heavily used bars.

Wildlife

Black bears live in parts of Arkansas. Store food securely and keep a clean camp.

Getting there from Texas

From North Texas, the Ouachitas and Hot Springs are a long day's drive, and the Buffalo River country sits farther north. Many Texas campers split the trip with a night in southeast Oklahoma or southwest Arkansas. Fill up and stock groceries in larger towns before heading into the hills, since stores thin out in the mountains and mountain roads are slower than the map suggests. Curvy two-lane highways with steep grades are the norm, so if you are towing a trailer, plan shorter driving days and use lower gears on descents.

Choosing a base camp

The Ozarks reward a base-camp style trip. Pick one campground with good access to a river, a few trailheads and a town for supplies, then spend several days exploring from there. That saves you from breaking camp every morning and lets you settle into a rhythm.

When you compare campgrounds, look at:

  • River access: can you launch a canoe or kayak nearby, or arrange a shuttle?
  • Shade: summer humidity makes shade more valuable than a view.
  • Elevation: ridge-top campgrounds catch breezes; hollows are cooler at night but damp.
  • Cell coverage: often spotty in deep valleys, so download maps in advance.

Float trip step by step

  1. Pick a section. Ask a local outfitter or the managing agency which stretches are floatable at the current water level.
  2. Arrange the shuttle. Outfitters commonly run shuttles; with two vehicles you can run your own.
  3. Pack the boat. Dry bags tied in, heavy items low, a cooler wedged in the center of a canoe.
  4. Launch early. Mornings are cooler and calmer, and you will have time to pick a good gravel bar.
  5. Scout unknown riffles. Bluff pools and gentle riffles are common, but downed trees can block channels after floods.
  6. Camp high. Choose a spot on the gravel bar well above the waterline and away from the main current.
  7. Take out on time. Shuttle drivers and daylight both run on a schedule.

Gravel bar kitchen setup

Gravel bars make a great natural kitchen floor, but they come with a few quirks. Loose rock shifts, so level the grill by working it down into the gravel rather than propping it up on stacked stones that can tip. Keep the cook area a fair distance from your tent and from any driftwood piles, which can be very dry and catch embers. A raised steel grill is the right tool here: coals stay contained instead of scorching the rocks, and you pack the cold ash out the next morning instead of leaving the black rings that dot heavily used bars.

Gravel bar menu

  • Supper: smoked sausage, peppers and onions over the grate, with skillet potatoes.
  • Breakfast: griddle pancakes and bacon before you load the boats.
  • Lunch on the water: tortillas, cheese, summer sausage, apples.
  • Second supper: chicken thighs frozen at home, thawed in the cooler by day two.

Hiking safety in the hills

Ozark trails often drop steeply into hollows and climb back out. Allow extra time, carry more water than you think you need in summer, and wear shoes with solid grip for wet rock near waterfalls. Bluff edges are often unfenced; keep kids and dogs close. Ticks and chiggers are common in grass and brush, so use repellent and check yourself every evening.

Troubleshooting Ozark trips

  • River too low: pick a section closer to the headwaters' big springs or a lower stretch with more flow, or swap to a hiking day.
  • River rising: move camp higher at once and do not launch into muddy, fast water.
  • Everything is damp: hang wet clothes under a tarp in the breeze and keep a dry sleep set sealed in a bag.
  • Firewood rules: buy firewood locally where required, or bring charcoal and skip the question entirely.

Leave the bar better

Popular gravel bars get heavy summer use. Pick up any litter you find, carry out all your ash and food scraps, and never bury trash in the gravel; the next flood will just carry it downstream.

Floating and camping

Many Ozark rivers are popular for float trips. Check flow levels, follow local rules, and camp only where permitted, such as designated gravel bars or campgrounds.

Hiking

The Ozarks offer forest trails, bluffs and waterfalls, especially after rain.

Cooking

Set the grill on gravel or a pad, follow fire rules, and clean up thoroughly.

Seasons in the Ozarks

Spring brings wildflowers and waterfalls, summer is hot and humid, fall brings colorful leaves, and winter is quiet with cold nights.

Ozark camp cooking ideas

Fresh trout from a licensed catch or a local market grills beautifully over medium coals with lemon and butter. Smoked sausage, grilled corn and skillet potatoes make a hearty supper after a long float. For dessert, a skillet cobbler made with local peaches or blackberries in summer is hard to beat. Keep coals contained in a raised grill on a gravel bar or pad, and carry out the cold ash where no disposal is provided.

Respecting private land

Many Ozark riverbanks border private property. Stay on public land and designated sites, and ask permission before crossing or camping on private land.

A final note

Ozark weather can change quickly, especially in spring. Watch for storms that raise rivers fast, and move camp to higher ground if water starts rising near your site.

Questions campers ask

What is the Buffalo National River known for?

It was the first national river in the United States and is known for bluffs, float trips and gravel bar camping.

Is there camping at Hot Springs National Park?

The park has a campground; check current details with the park.

When is the best time to visit the Ozarks?

Fall for color and spring for waterfalls; summer for floating.
