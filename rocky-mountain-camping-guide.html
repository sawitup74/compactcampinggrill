Camping in the Rocky Mountains

High-elevation camping in Colorado, Wyoming, Montana and beyond: altitude, weather, wildlife and the parks Texans love.

For many Texans, the Rocky Mountains are the summer escape: cool nights, alpine lakes, aspen groves and peaks well above anything back home. Camping in the Rockies is unforgettable, but altitude, rapidly changing weather and wildlife demand respect.

Rocky Mountain National Park

In northern Colorado, the park is known for high peaks, alpine tundra, wildflowers and large herds of elk. Trail Ridge Road climbs above treeline and is a famous scenic drive when open in summer. Campgrounds are popular and fill early in summer.

Yellowstone and Grand Teton

In northwest Wyoming, Yellowstone is the world's first national park and is known for geysers, hot springs and wildlife such as bison, elk, wolves and grizzly bears. Just south, Grand Teton National Park offers jagged peaks rising above lakes and the Snake River. Both parks are extremely popular in summer.

Glacier National Park

In northern Montana, Glacier is known for rugged peaks, glacially carved valleys, lakes and the Going-to-the-Sun Road. The season is short, and many facilities open only in summer.

Colorado's national forests and high country

Colorado has extensive national forest land with campgrounds and dispersed camping where permitted. Mountain towns provide supplies and a base for exploring.

New Mexico's southern Rockies

The Sangre de Cristo Mountains reach into northern New Mexico, closer to Texas. National forests there offer high-elevation camping without driving as far north.

Altitude

Many Rocky Mountain campgrounds sit at high elevation. Altitude can cause headaches, nausea, fatigue and poor sleep.

  • Ascend gradually if possible; spend a night at a middle elevation.
  • Drink plenty of water.
  • Go easy on alcohol the first few days.
  • Take it slow on hikes.
  • Descend if symptoms become severe.

Food also cooks differently at altitude. Water boils at a lower temperature, so pasta and rice take longer. Grilling is less affected, but expect charcoal to behave a little differently in thin, dry air and windy conditions.

Weather

  • Afternoon thunderstorms are common in summer. Start hikes early and be off exposed ridges by early afternoon.
  • Cold nights: even in July, nights can be cold at high elevations. Pack warm layers and a sleeping bag rated well below freezing for high camps.
  • Snow can fall in any month at the highest elevations.
  • Sun: UV is intense at altitude. Use sunscreen and sunglasses.

Wildlife

  • Elk and moose: give them plenty of space, especially in rut and calving seasons. Moose can be aggressive.
  • Bears: black bears live throughout the Rockies, and grizzlies live in parts of the northern Rockies. Follow all food storage rules, and carry bear spray where recommended.
  • Bison in Yellowstone are dangerous; keep your distance.

Fire rules in the Rockies

Dry forests and wind make fire danger serious. Fire restrictions are common in summer and can ban all open fires, including charcoal in some cases. Many areas also restrict firewood movement. Check current restrictions daily.

Where charcoal grills are permitted, a raised steel grill on a durable surface gives you a contained cook fire. When restrictions tighten, follow them without exception.

Packing for the mountains

  • Warm sleeping bag and insulated pad
  • Insulated jacket, hat and gloves, even in summer
  • Rain gear
  • Sun protection
  • Extra water and electrolytes
  • Bear-resistant storage as required
  • Flat-pack grill and charcoal in a sealed container

Mountain camp menu

  • Grilled steaks or elk burgers (game where legally obtained)
  • Foil-free trout over coals
  • Hearty breakfast skillets for cold mornings
  • Hot drinks heated on the edge of the grill

Driving to the Rockies from Texas

The drive is long. Break it up with camping in the Texas Panhandle or northern New Mexico. Check road conditions for mountain passes; some close seasonally.

Leave No Trace at altitude

Alpine plants grow slowly and are easily damaged. Stay on trails, camp on durable surfaces, and pack out everything.

Setting up a high-elevation camp

  1. Arrive early in the day. Afternoon storms are common in summer, and you want the tent up before they build.
  2. Pick a sheltered site. Trees or terrain can block wind, but avoid spots under dead trees.
  3. Stake and guy out fully. Mountain wind can come up suddenly at night.
  4. Set up the kitchen downwind and away from the tent, especially in bear country.
  5. Secure food immediately in a vehicle, locker or bear canister as required.
  6. Lay out warm layers before dark; temperatures fall quickly after sunset.

Cooking in thin, cold air

  • Charcoal takes longer to come up to heat on cold, windy evenings. Light it earlier than you would at home and use a chimney to get it going.
  • Shelter the grill from wind with your vehicle or a rock outcrop, keeping it on a durable, non-combustible surface and clear of anything flammable.
  • Boiling takes longer at altitude, and pasta and rice need extra time. Grilled meats and vegetables are much less affected.
  • Keep a lid or foil tent handy to hold heat around food on chilly nights.

Rocky Mountain camp breakfasts

Cold mornings call for a hot breakfast. A griddle breakfast of eggs, potatoes and bacon, or a skillet hash, warms everyone up fast. Heat water for coffee and cocoa on the edge of the grill while breakfast cooks.

Hiking safety

  • Start early and be off summits and exposed ridges before afternoon storms.
  • Carry rain gear and a warm layer even on sunny mornings.
  • Drink water steadily; the dry air dehydrates you faster.
  • Know your turnaround time and stick to it.
  • Watch for altitude symptoms and descend if they worsen.

Wildlife viewing ethics

The Rockies offer incredible wildlife viewing, from elk bugling in fall to moose in willow thickets. Watch from a distance, use binoculars, and never approach for a photo. Pull fully off the road if you stop, and never block traffic.

Seasonal guide

  • Late spring: snow lingers at high elevations; lower trails open first.
  • Summer: peak season for high-country camping; afternoon storms are common.
  • Fall: golden aspens and elk rut, cold nights, fewer crowds after Labor Day.
  • Winter: many high roads and campgrounds close; winter camping requires special skills and gear.

Troubleshooting mountain trips

  • Headache and nausea at camp: likely altitude; rest, drink water and descend if it does not improve.
  • Gear soaked by afternoon rain: pitch a tarp over the kitchen and keep sleeping bags in dry bags.
  • Campground full: look for nearby national forest campgrounds and dispersed camping where permitted.
  • Fire ban announced: follow it. Switch to no-cook meals or a stove if allowed.

A note on dispersed camping

Many national forest lands in the Rockies allow dispersed camping outside developed campgrounds, subject to local rules. Use existing sites, stay the permitted number of days, camp well away from water, and pack out everything. Fire rules apply here too and are often stricter.

Packing the grill for mountain trips

A flat-pack grill rides easily in a car or truck on a long drive north from Texas. At camp, set it on gravel or rock, keep it well away from dry grass and timber, and keep water within reach. When restrictions allow charcoal, you get a contained cook fire on clean steel instead of a shared ring.

Food storage in bear country

Many Rocky Mountain campgrounds require food, coolers, trash and scented items to be stored in bear-resistant lockers or hard-sided vehicles. Follow posted rules exactly, keep a clean camp, and never leave food unattended, even for a few minutes.

Leaving room for the unexpected

Mountain trips rarely go exactly to plan. Roads close for weather, campgrounds fill, and storms change hiking plans. Build slack into your itinerary, keep a list of backup campgrounds, and have a few no-cook meals ready for days when the weather won't cooperate.

Sunscreen at altitude

UV exposure increases at higher elevations. Wear sunscreen, a hat and sunglasses even on cool or cloudy days, and reapply often.

Mountain lakes

Alpine lakes are stunning but very cold. Wade carefully, watch kids closely, and dry off quickly in the wind. Camp at designated sites back from the shore, where rules require it, to protect fragile lakeside vegetation.

A final note

Carry a small bag of salty snacks on hikes to replace what you lose sweating in dry mountain air.

Questions campers ask

How do I prevent altitude sickness while camping?

Ascend gradually, drink plenty of water, limit alcohol and take it easy for the first few days.

Are fires allowed in the Rocky Mountains?

It depends on current restrictions, which are common in summer. Check before you go.

How cold does it get at night in the Rockies in summer?

Nights can be cold at high elevations even in July, so pack warm layers and a warm sleeping bag.
