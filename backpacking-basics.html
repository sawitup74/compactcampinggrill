Backpacking Basics for Car Campers Ready to Walk In

What changes when you carry everything on your back: the big three, water, food, navigation and a first overnight plan.

At some point many car campers look at a trail disappearing into the trees and wonder what is out there. Backpacking is the answer, and it is less intimidating than it looks. You are still camping. You simply carry a smaller, lighter version of your camp. This guide covers what changes and how to plan a gentle first overnight.

The big three

Your heaviest items are shelter, sleep system and pack. Getting these lighter makes the biggest difference.

  • Shelter: a one- or two-person backpacking tent, or a tarp setup for experienced campers.
  • Sleep system: a compressible bag and an inflatable or foam pad.
  • Pack: sized to your torso, typically 40 to 65 liters for overnight to multi-day trips. Have it fitted if possible.

Water

Water is heavy, about two pounds per liter. Instead of carrying it all, plan around sources on the map and treat what you collect.

  • Filters, chemical tablets, UV pens and boiling all work for treating water.
  • Carry enough to reach the next reliable source, plus a margin.
  • In dry country, sources can vanish in summer. Ask a ranger about current conditions.

Food on the trail

Backpacking food trades fresh for light. Think calorie-dense and shelf-stable: tortillas, nut butter, hard cheese, jerky, dried fruit, instant rice and dehydrated meals. A small stove is the common backpacking cook system. Many backcountry areas restrict or ban open fires, so plan to cook without one.

Where does a grill fit? Usually at the trailhead and at basecamp-style trips. Many backpackers love a cooked steak and a small fire on the night before a hike or the night they come out. A compact grill living in the car is a great reward waiting at the end. For short walk-ins to established campsites, a lightweight steel grill can make the trip if fires are allowed, but on true multi-day treks, weight wins.

Navigation

Carry a map of the area and know how to read it. Phone apps with downloaded offline maps are helpful, but batteries die. A compass and basic skills are worth learning. Tell someone your route and return time.

Clothing layers

  • Base layer that wicks moisture
  • Insulating layer (fleece or puffy)
  • Rain shell
  • Extra socks and a warm hat
  • Avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold

The ten essentials

Hiking groups recommend carrying these ten systems on any trip into the backcountry:

  1. Navigation
  2. Headlamp
  3. Sun protection
  4. First aid
  5. Knife or multi-tool
  6. Fire starting materials
  7. Emergency shelter
  8. Extra food
  9. Extra water
  10. Extra clothes

Your first overnight

Pick a trail of two to five miles with a known campsite, modest elevation gain, and water nearby. Go in good weather. Start early so you arrive with plenty of daylight to set up camp. Many state parks have walk-in or primitive sites that make excellent first backpacking trips; ask the park about permits and fire rules.

Leave No Trace matters more out here

Backcountry areas get fewer cleanup crews. Pack out everything, including food scraps. Use existing campsites. Dig catholes far from water. Keep fires small or skip them entirely where rules or conditions advise.

Trailhead feast

Here is a tradition worth starting: leave a flat-pack grill, charcoal, a lighter and a cooler of something good in the car. When you come off the trail tired and hungry, a hot meal at the trailhead parking area (where permitted) or your next campground is the best part of the trip.

Fitting a pack

  1. Measure torso length.
  2. Adjust the hip belt so it rests on your hip bones.
  3. Tighten shoulder straps.
  4. Adjust load lifters.
  5. Fasten the sternum strap.

Most of the weight should rest on your hips.

Packing the pack

  • Bottom: sleeping bag
  • Middle, close to back: heavy items like food and water
  • Top: items needed during the day
  • Outer pockets: snacks, map, sunscreen

Training

Walk with a loaded pack before your trip. Build up distance gradually.

Trail etiquette

  • Hikers going uphill have the right of way.
  • Yield to horses.
  • Stay on the trail.
  • Keep noise low.

Foot care

  • Wear broken-in boots.
  • Use good socks.
  • Treat hot spots before they become blisters.

Water treatment details

  • Filters: remove bacteria and protozoa.
  • Purifiers: also remove viruses.
  • Chemical treatment: follow directions for contact time.
  • Boiling: a rolling boil kills pathogens.

First backpacking trip menu

  • Dinner: dehydrated meal or instant rice with tuna
  • Breakfast: oatmeal with dried fruit
  • Lunch: tortilla with peanut butter
  • Snacks: trail mix and bars

Bear country

Use bear canisters or hangs as required. Cook and store food away from your tent.

Building up to a first trip

  1. Day hikes with a loaded pack. Learn how weight feels on your shoulders and hips.
  2. A short overnight close to the trailhead.
  3. A longer trip once you trust your gear and skills.

The big three

Your pack, shelter and sleep system are the heaviest items. Lighter versions of these make the biggest difference in comfort.

Packing your pack

  • Sleeping bag at the bottom.
  • Heavy items (food, water, cook kit) close to your back, middle of the pack.
  • Frequently used items (snacks, rain jacket, map) in top or hip pockets.
  • Adjust hip belt to carry most of the weight on your hips.

Water

Know where water sources are on your route, and filter or treat all water. Carry enough between sources, especially in dry areas.

Food

Plan high-calorie, lightweight food. Repack to remove bulk. Store food according to local rules.

Cooking on the trail

Backpackers usually cook on small stoves. For base-camp style trips with a short walk in, a compact grill can come along for a real cooked meal at camp.

Navigation

Carry a map and compass and know how to use them, even if you have a GPS or phone app.

Leave No Trace

Camp on durable surfaces, pack out all trash, and dispose of waste properly.

Safety

Tell someone your plans, carry a first aid kit, and know when to turn back.

Trail etiquette

Yield to uphill hikers, step aside for horses, and keep noise down.

A beginner's gear list

  • Backpack that fits your torso length
  • Lightweight tent or shelter
  • Sleeping bag and pad
  • Small stove, fuel and pot
  • Water filter or treatment
  • Headlamp
  • Map and compass
  • First aid kit
  • Rain jacket and warm layer
  • Knife or multi-tool
  • Sun protection
  • Food and a way to store it

Foot care

Blisters end more trips than anything else. Break in boots before the trip, wear wool or synthetic socks, and stop to treat hot spots right away with tape or blister pads. Air your feet at breaks and change into dry socks at camp.

Pacing

Hike at a pace where you can still hold a conversation. Take short breaks every hour to eat and drink. Hills are easier with shorter steps.

Choosing a campsite on the trail

Camp at established sites or designated spots, at least 200 feet from water where rules require it. Look for flat, durable ground, protection from wind, and no dead trees overhead.

Base camp backpacking

For a hybrid trip, hike a short distance to a base camp, set up, and day-hike from there with a light pack. A short carry like this can make a compact grill reasonable to bring for real grilled meals at camp, where fires or charcoal are allowed.

Pack weight goals

A common guideline is to keep a loaded pack at or below about a fifth of your body weight for comfort. Weigh your pack at home and look for items to leave behind or swap for lighter versions.

Your first night on the trail

Arrive at camp with plenty of daylight. Set up the tent first, then filter water, then cook. Hang or store food properly before dark. Get into dry clothes and warm up before bed. Expect to sleep lightly; it gets easier each night.

One more tip

Practice skills like pitching your tent, filtering water and using your stove at home or on day hikes before relying on them in the backcountry.

Questions campers ask

How heavy should my backpacking pack be?

Many hikers aim for a total weight around 20 percent of body weight or less for comfort, though it varies.

Can I have a campfire while backpacking?

It depends on the area. Many backcountry zones restrict fires; check rules and conditions.

Is a grill practical for backpacking?

For long treks, usually not. For short walk-ins to established sites or trailhead meals, a compact grill can be worth it.
